**Innovative learning models and their syntaxes** refer to student-centered pedagogical frameworks equipped with standard operational steps in the classroom. The four main models recommended in modern curricula are Problem-Based Learning (PBL), Project-Based Learning (PjBL), Discovery Learning, and Inquiry-Based Learning, plus modern hybrid models like the Flipped Classroom.

Many lesson plans look great on paper but fall apart when teachers execute them in class. We often feel tempted to include modern-sounding model names just to satisfy administrative supervision, without dissecting their operational workflows.

The reality is clear. Without mastering the precise sequence of steps, group discussions easily turn into noise with no measurable learning outcomes.

## Basic Concepts: Why Does Syntax Determine Classroom Success?

According to pedagogical experts Bruce Joyce and Marsha Weil in their book *Models of Teaching*, a learning model is a conceptual framework describing a systematic procedure for organizing learning experiences to achieve specific goals. This framework serves as a roadmap for curriculum designers and teachers when planning teaching and learning activities.

If you still confuse conceptual umbrella terms with practical teaching methods, review the [differences between learning approaches, models, strategies, methods, and techniques](/blog/differences-approach-model-strategy-method-technique-learning) to keep your lesson plan foundation accurate.

A model is categorized as "innovative" in modern paradigms if it meets four main indicators:

* **Student-centered**: The teacher acts as a facilitator and provides scaffolding, rather than being the only source of information.
* **Sharpens 21st-century skills (4C)**: Actively integrates critical thinking, creativity, collaboration, and communication.
* **Stimulates higher-order thinking skills (HOTS)**: Pushes student cognition to the analyzing (C4), evaluating (C5), and creating (C6) levels in Anderson and Krathwohl's revised Bloom's Taxonomy.
* **Meaningful and contextual**: Connects textbook materials with students' daily realities to foster metacognitive awareness.

Inside every learning model is a key element called **syntax**. Syntax is the standard sequence or stage of operational activities (learning phases). This element distinguishes one model from another while guiding teacher behavior and student activity from the start to the end of a learning session. Skipping a syntax phase is like breaking the chain of the student's cognitive construction.

## Innovative Learning Models and Their Syntaxes

Assessment and learning guidelines generally establish four main models with a constructivist paradigm, plus classroom management innovations like the Flipped Classroom. Here is a complete breakdown of various innovative learning models and their syntaxes that you can apply immediately.

### 1. Problem-Based Learning (PBL)

Rooted in the constructivist theory developed by Howard Barrows and Richard I. Arends, Problem-Based Learning uses open-ended, real-world problems (ill-structured problems) as the starting point for learning. Students do not just solve word problems; they investigate authentic dilemmas that lack a single correct answer.

The standard PBL syntax consists of 5 operational phases according to Arends:

1. **Orient students to the problem**: The teacher presents a contradictory phenomenon or a real case around the school, such as a video on clean water scarcity or data on local market waste spikes. Students observe and identify the core issue.
2. **Organize students for study**: The teacher divides students into small groups of 4 to 5 people. Each group formulates the boundaries of the problem they will investigate and divides work roles among team members.
3. **Assist independent and group investigation**: Students collect information through literature studies, field observations, resource person interviews, or simple experiments. The teacher walks around providing guiding questions to maintain investigative focus.
4. **Develop and present artifacts and exhibits**: Groups compile solution artifacts (like recommendation drafts, problem-solving flow infographics, or simple water purifier prototypes) and present them to other groups.
5. **Analyze and evaluate the problem-solving process**: The teacher and students critically reflect on data accuracy, the logic of the proposed solutions, and thinking constraints during the investigation process.

For example implementation plans and case studies across various grade levels, you can study the specific guide on [Problem Based Learning stages and practical syntax](/blog/problem-based-learning-stages-and-syntax).

### 2. Project-Based Learning (PjBL)

Based on John Dewey's "learning by doing" philosophy and developed by the Lucas Educational Foundation, PjBL focuses on creating concrete products through deep investigation over a scheduled timeframe.

::warning
Do not confuse PjBL with standard crafts or handiwork assignments. Asking students to make a cardboard miniature house without prior scientific problem formulation, concept investigation, and product function testing is not PjBL, but merely a motor skills task.
::

The standard PjBL syntax includes 6 systematic stages:

1. **Start with the Essential Question**: The teacher sparks student curiosity with a challenging question that requires creating a product as a solution. For example: "How can we process fruit peel waste in the village market into an eco-friendly cleaning liquid?"
2. **Design a Plan for the Project**: Students and the teacher design project specifications, determine the list of tools and materials, agree on work rules, and select scientific reference sources.
3. **Create a Schedule**: The group creates a timeline containing target deadlines for each project stage, from initial experiments to final product completion.
4. **Monitor the Students and the Progress of the Project**: Students execute the project creation. The teacher acts as a mentor who checks group work journals, monitors collaboration, and provides formative feedback.
5. **Assess the Outcome**: The group presents and tests the feasibility of the product's function in front of the class or a real audience. The teacher assesses the quality of the work and the achievement of learning objectives using an authentic rubric.
6. **Evaluate the Experience**: Students and the teacher conduct a complete reflection on team dynamics, technical obstacles they successfully overcame, and improvements for the next project.

If you want to build a measurable project assessment rubric, please refer to the [Project Based Learning guide and PjBL syntax](/blog/project-based-learning-guide-and-syntax).

### 3. Discovery Learning

Conceived by cognitive psychologist Jerome Bruner, Discovery Learning directs students to discover a concept, pattern, grammar rule, or science formula themselves through inductive reasoning. The teacher does not present definitions or formulas at the beginning of the lesson, but provides structured examples so students can conclude the general rules.

The standard Discovery Learning syntax includes 6 phases:

1. **Stimulation**: The teacher presents anomalous phenomena, pictures, number tables, or short demonstrations that raise questions without giving the final concept explanation.
2. **Problem Statement**: Students choose a focus question from the phenomenon and formulate a hypothesis or temporary guess.
3. **Data Collection**: Students conduct active exploration, such as measuring flat shapes, observing literature, or dissecting text structures to gather evidence.
4. **Data Processing**: Students classify, calculate, and interpret the collected data to find recurring patterns.
5. **Verification**: Students double-check whether their initial hypothesis matches and proves true when compared to the data processing results.
6. **Generalization**: Students formulate basic principles, concept definitions, or general formulas using their own words under the teacher's confirmation.

### 4. Inquiry-Based Learning

Developed by educational figures like Richard Suchman, the inquiry model places students like young researchers. At first glance, this model looks similar to Discovery Learning, but it has a fundamental difference in the nature of its investigation. Discovery Learning guides students to rediscover standard curriculum concepts inductively, while Inquiry-Based Learning emphasizes formal scientific method procedures to test cause-and-effect variables objectively.

The standard Inquiry-Based Learning syntax consists of 5 stages:

1. **Orientation**: The teacher builds an exploration climate, explains the topic of natural or social phenomena, and outlines the scope of the observation procedures to be taken.
2. **Formulating the problem**: Students compile clear research questions and determine the cause-and-effect variables to be studied.
3. **Formulating hypotheses**: Students formulate logical temporary scientific guesses based on their prior knowledge or basic theories.
4. **Collecting data or experimenting**: Students design and run laboratory tests, structured observations, or factual surveys to test variables.
5. **Testing hypotheses and formulating conclusions**: Students analyze empirical evidence to accept or reject the initial hypothesis, then establish their scientific conclusion.

You can read more about the technical comparison of these two discovery models in the article on [Discovery Learning and Inquiry stages for class](/blog/discovery-learning-and-inquiry-stages-for-class).

### 5. Flipped Classroom

Popularized by education practitioners Jonathan Bergmann and Aaron Sams, the Flipped Classroom reverses the traditional class structure. The teacher moves basic material information transfer outside school hours through independent learning media, so face-to-face class time is fully used for active discussion, directed guidance, and complex problem-solving.

The Flipped Classroom workflow is divided into 3 continuous cycles:

1. **Before Class (Asynchronous)**: Students study basic materials independently at home through interactive micro-videos or essential readings, then answer brief diagnostic assessments.
2. **During Class (Synchronous Face-to-Face)**: The teacher no longer lectures to explain basic definitions. Class time is immediately used to dissect misconceptions from diagnostic quizzes, deep case simulations, or differentiated group guidance.
3. **After Class**: Students complete enrichment assignments, evaluate learning achievements, and document reflections in their digital portfolios.

When compiling a Lesson Plan, you need to map out the various innovative learning models and their syntaxes to align with curriculum targets. Pay attention to the characteristic comparison of these five models:

| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Problem-Based Learning (PBL)** | Constructivism (Barrows, Arends) | Social case analysis, environmental dilemmas, open problem-solving (C4 to C5) | Solution ideas, analysis reports, recommendation drafts | 2 to 3 Meetings |
| **Project-Based Learning (PjBL)** | Learning by Doing (John Dewey, Lucas Foundation) | Cross-disciplinary work creation, applied engineering, product design (C5 to C6) | Authentic physical products, functional prototypes, media campaigns | 2 to 4 Meetings |

| **Flipped Classroom** | Blended Learning (Bergmann & Sams) | Concept-dense materials needing plenty of applied practice time in class (C3 to C6) | Completion of complex case studies and digital portfolios | 1 to 2 Meeting Cycles |

## Syntax Integration with Deep Learning and Differentiation Approaches

Modern pedagogical policy directions prioritize the **Deep Learning** approach based on three pillars: Mindful Learning, Meaningful Learning, and Joyful Learning. Innovative learning models are not just lists of administrative sequences, but primary instruments to achieve deep conceptual understanding, rather than rote memorization.

When you run the learning model syntax in class, the three pillars of Deep Learning work together:

* **Mindful Learning** grows during the **problem orientation and final reflection phases**. As students observe real problems and evaluate their own thinking processes, they become fully aware of why the topic matters.
* **Meaningful Learning** forms during the **investigation, data collection, and evidence testing phases**. Students connect textbook concepts with real field contexts so new knowledge ties strongly to long-term memory.
* **Joyful Learning** appears during the **work design, active exploration, and result presentation phases**. Learning joy in the Deep Learning concept is not just clapping hands, but cognitive satisfaction when students successfully solve challenges or see their creations work.

Also, you do not need to create separate learning plan formats to apply **differentiated learning** based on Carol Ann Tomlinson's framework. Armed with initial assessment results (cognitive and non-cognitive diagnostic), insert the three differentiation elements directly into the syntax stages of the model you are using:

* **Content Differentiation (during Orientation & Data Collection Phases)**: Provide a variety of learning sources according to student readiness and learning profiles. Students with basic readiness can observe short videos or illustrated infographics, while more advanced students analyze article texts or statistical data tables.
* **Process Differentiation (during Investigation & Project Design Phases)**: Give different portions of scaffolding. Independent groups only need to follow work guide sheets, while beginner groups who still struggle to formulate hypotheses get step-by-step direct assistance.
* **Product Differentiation (during Result Presentation & Testing Phases)**: In PBL or PjBL models, allow groups to choose output formats matching their interests as long as they refer to the same Learning Objective rubric. Students may present solutions as podcasts, demonstration videos, comic posters, or written reports.

## Guide to Breaking Syntax Across Several Meetings

One of the most common traps teachers experience in the field is rigid administrative compliance with syntax. Many educators force all syntax stages to finish in a single meeting (for example, 2 Lesson Hours or 80 minutes). Forcing 6 PjBL stages to finish in 80 minutes only produces a shallow, rushed, and artificial inquiry process.

Design modular syntaxes across meetings in your Lesson Plan. Here are practical steps to divide Project-Based Learning (PjBL) syntax into three meetings:

::steps
### First Meeting: Building Foundation and Plan (Syntax 1 to 3)
Focus the first lesson hour on sparking student curiosity through essential questions (Syntax 1), dividing groups to plan product designs along with tool and material lists (Syntax 2), and creating project deadline schedules (Syntax 3). Students go home with a work plan already approved by the teacher.

### Second Meeting: Project Execution and Formative Assessment (Syntax 4)
Use the entire second lesson hour specifically for product creation in class or the laboratory. The teacher walks around to monitor project progress (Syntax 4), check group work journals, observe collaboration, and provide formative feedback when group designs are inaccurate.

### Third Meeting: Work Testing and Experience Reflection (Syntax 5 and 6)
Allocate the last meeting for presentation and product testing in front of the class using an authentic rubric (Syntax 5). End the module series with a learning experience evaluation session to reflect on constraints and teamwork (Syntax 6).
::

## Four Common Mistakes in Applying Learning Models in Class

Even though the model name is neatly written in planning documents, classroom execution often slips due to these four classic mistakes:

1. **Equating word problem practice with PBL**: Giving a worksheet full of routine math word problems ending in a question mark and asking students to solve them in groups is not Problem-Based Learning. Problems in PBL must be authentic, open-ended (ill-structured), relevant to student lives, and have various alternative ways to solve them.
2. **Allowing unequal group workloads**: In PjBL or PBL, often only one or two students work hard while other members stay passive. Fix this issue by limiting group members to a maximum of 4 to 5 people and setting specific investigation role divisions starting from the organizing phase.
3. **Teacher intervening too quickly when students are confused**: In Discovery Learning, when students look stuck while processing data, teachers often lose patience and write the formula or conclusion directly on the whiteboard. This action ruins the inductive discovery process. Provide guiding questions so students still find the pattern themselves.
4. **Piling up assessments only at the presentation end**: Many new teachers only bring out assessment instruments when students come forward to the front of the class in the last phase (summative product). Instead, you need to insert formative assessments like collaboration observations, peer feedback, and reflective notes at every syntax node, especially during the investigation and design phases.

::tip
Pick one main learning model for one Learning Objective unit and run its syntax completely until finished. Avoid mixing two large model syntaxes at once in a single meeting so students do not lose their learning direction.
::

::faq
  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: What is the fundamental difference between Problem-Based Learning (PBL) and Project-Based Learning (PjBL)?
  ---
  PBL focuses on the process of discovering solutions to complex contextual problems, where the final result can be recommendations, analysis, or ideas. In contrast, PjBL focuses on creating authentic concrete products within a specific timeframe designed to answer an essential question.
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: What is the difference between Discovery Learning and Inquiry Learning?
  ---
  Discovery Learning emphasizes finding concepts, patterns, or principles that are actually standard in the curriculum through inductive guidance. Meanwhile, Inquiry Learning emphasizes formal scientific investigation processes just like scientific researchers to test variables and prove hypotheses objectively.
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: How do I choose the right learning model for the Learning Achievement target?
  ---
  Match the Operational Verbs in your Learning Objective with the material characteristics. Objectives based on understanding concepts or basic rules fit well with Discovery Learning. Objectives based on analyzing social or science problems suit PBL, while those based on creating or designing work suit PjBL.
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: Do learning model syntaxes have to be written in the Lesson Plan?
  ---
  Yes. In the Core Activities section of your Lesson Plan, the teacher and student activity steps are highly recommended to explicitly and sequentially reflect the syntax phases of your chosen model.
  :::
::

::conclusion
Mastering various innovative learning models and their syntaxes will free you from exhausting one-way teaching routines. As a first step in planning next week's lessons, make decisions based on your material targets:

- **Use Problem-Based Learning (PBL)** when the subject matter raises environmental issues, social dilemmas, or contextual cases requiring students to argue for the best solution.
- **Use Project-Based Learning (PjBL)** when you have a time allocation of 2 to 4 meetings to guide students in designing, building, and testing real work.
- **Use Discovery or Inquiry Learning** when you want students to prove science concepts, math formulas, or language rules themselves through data observation and directed experiments.
::
