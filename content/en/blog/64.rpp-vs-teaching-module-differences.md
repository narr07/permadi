The **difference between an RPP (Lesson Plan) and a teaching module** lies in the completeness of their instruments and their primary users. An RPP is a step-by-step technical scenario for teachers containing three core components: objectives, learning activities, and assessments. In contrast, a teaching module is a complete learning package (an "RPP Plus") equipped with teaching materials, student worksheets (LKPD), detailed assessment rubrics, and differentiation guides.

As educators, we often see confusion in the teachers' lounge at the start of every new academic year. Has the RPP been abolished? Are we required to print dozens of pages of teaching modules for every meeting? These questions naturally arise due to the conflicting information circulating in the field.

The good news is that these two documents do not cancel each other out. Official regulations give teachers full flexibility to choose the format that best suits their readiness. To avoid taking the wrong step when preparing these instruments, we need to break down the anatomy, function, and shift in thinking behind these two planning documents.

## Understanding the Core Differences Between RPP and Merdeka Curriculum Teaching Modules

Many fellow teachers think the change in terminology from RPP to teaching module is just a curriculum rebranding. However, if we refer to the *2025 Revised Learning and Assessment Guide*, both are valid document options that stand side by side. A teaching module is essentially an RPP equipped with complete supporting instruments.

![Visual comparison diagram showing the structure of a concise lesson plan and an expanded teaching module](/article/edu-rpp-modul-ajar-ilustrasi.webp)

An RPP is designed as a concise guide for teachers when leading face-to-face classroom sessions. Since the teacher administration simplification policy took effect, the RPP format only needs to contain three core components: learning objectives, activity steps, and assessments. Teachers who have mastered the material and have adequate textbooks find this concise format very helpful.

So, why did teaching modules appear? Prof. Ade Tuti Rochayati Rosa in her book *Kurikulum Merdeka Belajar* explains that a teaching module is designed as a complete instrument to guide an independent, flexible, and student-centered learning process. This document serves as a teacher's guide while providing instruments that students use directly.

Inside a teaching module, the teaching scenario is directly combined with reading materials, Student Worksheets (LKPD), trigger questions, and reflection instruments. If you cannot attend class, a substitute teacher can directly run the lesson by reading one complete teaching module without needing to find a separate textbook.

::note
Schools and educators have full freedom to choose: compile a simple RPP (3 components) or use a complete teaching module. Both are officially recognized during administrative checks and performance observations.
::

### The Difference Between Syllabi, RPP, and Teaching Modules in Lesson Planning

To prevent overlap when preparing administrative documents, we also need to position the syllabus or Learning Objective Flow (ATP) alongside the RPP and teaching module. These three operate on different timeframes and technical depths.

In the 2013 Curriculum, we knew the syllabus as a macro plan for one semester or one year. In the Merdeka Curriculum, the role of this macro roadmap is replaced by the Learning Objective Flow (ATP) derived from the [Learning Outcomes (CP)](/blog/learning-outcomes-guide) per phase. Both the syllabus and ATP contain a broad outline of the material flow, a list of learning objectives, and a global allocation of study hours.

From that macro roadmap, teachers translate it into classroom implementation documents:

1. **Syllabus / ATP:** A macro roadmap for one year or one phase that maps the sequence of Learning Objectives (TP).
2. **RPP:** A micro face-to-face daily or weekly plan that details the teacher's teaching scenario concisely.
3. **Teaching Module:** A complete learning guide for one topic or one unit of Learning Objectives containing scenarios, reading materials, LKPD, media, and assessment rubrics in one ready-to-use bundle.

## A Shift in Thinking: From Mere Activities to Backward Design

If we only look at the table above, the difference between an RPP and a teaching module seems limited to the number of pages and appendices. However, the main differentiator actually lies in how we think when designing a lesson.

When compiling a conventional RPP, we often get trapped in a procedural sequence: "What activities should we do in class tomorrow?" We get busy choosing fun games, playing videos, or dividing discussion groups, only thinking about the test questions at the very end. Grant Wiggins and Jay McTighe in their classic book, *Understanding by Design*, call this trap an activity-oriented design (a design focused on activities, not on evidence of understanding).

The *2025 Revised Learning and Assessment Guide* recommends educators use a backward design framework when planning lessons. Instead of immediately writing activity steps, we are asked to think backward through three stages:

1. **Identify the desired results:** What long-term enduring understanding must students take home?
2. **Determine assessment evidence:** How do we know students understand? What performance rubric or product serves as evidence?
3. **Design learning activities:** What activities are most appropriate to guide students to achieve that evidence of understanding?

Teaching modules are specifically designed to guard this backward design mindset. That is why the core components of a teaching module include **Meaningful Understanding** and **Trigger Questions** before we enter the activity steps.

The completeness of the teaching module also reduces teacher dominance. With structured reading materials and explorative LKPDs inside the module, the classroom stage moves from one-way instruction to independent or collaborative exploration by students.

What about time efficiency in class? David R. Krathwohl in *A Revision of Bloom's Taxonomy* explains that objectives, activities, and assessments must align to increase effective time on task. A teaching module that prepares [differentiated instruction](/blog/differentiated-instruction-guide) scenarios from the start helps us provide scaffolding for slower students as well as additional challenges for faster students, without having to sacrifice extra lesson hours.

## The Difference Between RPP and Deep Learning Teaching Modules (RPM)

Entering the 2025 and 2026 academic years, we often hear a new term among educators: Deep Learning RPP or RPM (Deep Learning Plan). Many fellow teachers ask again, is this a new document format that replaces the teaching module?

The answer is no. The term Deep Learning RPP or teaching module is not a new bureaucratic document type that invalidates previous formats. This term refers to sharpening the content of lesson plans as the Ministry of Primary and Secondary Education (Kemendikdasmen) implements the Deep Learning strategy.

In the *2025 Revised Learning and Assessment Guide*, every lesson plan document, whether you choose the concise RPP form or a complete teaching module, must contain the principles of deep learning, both written and implied. What are the main characteristics?

First, the learning atmosphere must be built on three principles of learning experience:
* **Mindful:** Students are fully present, focused, and aware of the purpose of why they are studying the topic.
* **Meaningful:** Lesson material is tied directly to real-life problems around the students, not just memorized definitions.
* **Joyful:** The classroom environment is psychologically safe, challenges curiosity, and is free from the fear of being wrong.

Second, the syntax or activity steps in both the RPP and teaching module need to guide students through a complete cognitive cycle: **understanding** basic concepts, **applying** those concepts to real situations, and **reflecting** on their learning process metacognitively.

So, when we discuss the difference between an RPP and a deep learning teaching module, the differentiator remains the completeness of the appendices (3 components vs a complete package), but both are now equally required to drive the cycle of *understanding, applying, and reflecting* within their learning steps.

## Practical Steps to Create a Backward Design Teaching Module

For those of you wanting to transition from a procedural RPP to a teaching module focused on deep understanding, you do not need to create a document dozens of pages thick. Follow these three practical backward design stages.

::steps
### Stage 1: Determine Final Evidence and Meaningful Understanding
Start from the Learning Objectives (TP) found in the ATP. Before thinking about teaching methods, formulate an **Enduring Understanding**. This is not a formula or definition in a textbook, but the real-world benefit of the concept. After that, create open-ended **Trigger Questions** to provoke student curiosity. For example: *"Why does organic waste in the market rot faster than plastic waste in the cafeteria?"*

### Stage 2: Design Integrated Assessments From the Start
Do not place the test design at the very end. Prepare three layers of assessment that align with the objectives. First, design a short diagnostic assessment at the start of the meeting to map student readiness. Second, determine the form of [formative assessment](/blog/formative-assessment-in-classroom) (such as a discussion observation sheet or performance rubric) to monitor achievement during the process. Third, establish the criteria for [summative assessment](/blog/summative-assessment-guide) at the end of the module unit to ensure the TP targets are met.

### Stage 3: Arrange Differentiated Scenarios and Attach LKPD
After the objectives and measurement tools are ready, you can compile the activity steps (Introduction, Core, Closing) flowing from the *understanding*, *applying*, to *reflecting* phases. Include simple differentiation options: for example, groups still needing guidance get sequential work steps, while advanced groups receive open case studies. Finally, attach the LKPD and short reading materials that students will use directly during those core activities.
::

## Common Mistakes When Preparing RPP and Teaching Modules

In teacher learning community discussions, there are three classic misunderstandings that often drain our energy unnecessarily when preparing teaching instruments.

::warning
Avoid copying teaching modules raw from online platforms just to check an administrative box. Documents that do not match the reality of the students in your class will only make things difficult for you when teaching and being observed.
::

**1. Assuming Teaching Modules Must Be Dozens of Pages Thick**
Many teachers are reluctant to hear the word teaching module because they imagine documents 20 to 30 pages long per meeting. In reality, no rule dictates a specific page count. A teaching module appears longer purely because it attaches LKPDs and student reading materials. If for one topic you only need a few pages covering the objectives, activity steps, a short rubric, and one functional student worksheet, that document is validly called a teaching module. If you are not ready to prepare the appendices, simply use the much more concise 3-component RPP format.

**2. Getting Trapped in Fun Activities Without Core Understanding**
Often we design interactive quizzes or lively group games in an RPP, but when asked what concept is being tested, we are confused about how to answer. Ensure every physical activity or discussion in class always connects directly to the learning objectives and is measurable through the formative assessment instruments you have prepared.

**3. Using Downloaded Teaching Modules Without Contextualization**
Downloading teaching module references from official ministry platforms helps us avoid starting from a blank page. However, apply the ATM principle (Observe, Imitate, Modify). A teaching module made by a teacher at another school certainly cannot be used as is if the student profiles and school facilities differ. Adjust the case studies, language on the LKPD, and teaching media to your students' daily environment.

## When Should You Choose an RPP and When to Choose a Teaching Module?

After understanding the differences between the two, you might ask: "So for next semester, should I just make an RPP or a teaching module?"

Use this practical guide to make decisions at your school:

* **Choose a Simple RPP (3 Components) if:** The material you are teaching is well covered in the main textbooks (Student Book and Teacher Book), your preparation time is limited, and the students in your class already have their own textbooks. You simply need to write down the objectives, activity scenarios referencing textbook pages, and how to grade them.
* **Choose a Complete Teaching Module if:** You are teaching a new topic not yet discussed deeply in the textbook, applying a problem-based or project-based learning model (such as [Problem Based Learning](/blog/problem-based-learning-stages-and-syntax) or PjBL) that requires specific worksheets, or if your students' learning readiness varies significantly, requiring tiered teaching materials.
