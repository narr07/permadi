The history of curriculum development in Indonesia from 1947 to the Merdeka Curriculum represents a series of systemic changes to the national educational framework, learning goals, and teaching methodologies, which have changed 11 times. This evolution shifts the educational focus from ideological decolonization and rote memorization toward competency mastery, school autonomy, and student-centered deep learning.

For teachers in the classroom every day, frequent curriculum changes often cause administrative fatigue. Syllabus terms change, lesson plan formats are overhauled, and new training sessions keep coming.

However, viewing curriculum dynamics purely as a bureaucratic routine obscures its pedagogical essence. Each curriculum revision actually corrects the gaps of the previous one while responding to changing times.

::note
Broadly speaking, the journey of the national curriculum falls into four phases: the **Emancipation and National Ideology Phase (1947-1968)**, the **Instructional and Learning Efficiency Phase (1975-1994)**, the **Competency-Based and Decentralization Phase (2004-2013)**, and the **Flexibility and Deep Learning Phase (Merdeka Curriculum)**.
::

## Why Does the Indonesian Curriculum Keep Changing?

A curriculum is not a dead document set in stone. It is a pedagogical blueprint that must adapt to scientific advancements, shifts in learning psychology, and the needs of the professional world.

When we examine the history of curriculum development in Indonesia through the lens of instructional design, a clear dialectical cycle appears. When one curriculum becomes too centralized and rigid, the next one arrives bringing a spirit of autonomy and student activity. Conversely, when decentralization causes quality gaps between schools, the government steps back in to arrange more measurable national standards.

These changes also reflect the progression of learning theories in the classroom. We move from a behaviorist approach that relies on stimulus-response and material completion, toward constructivist and humanistic approaches that value reasoning processes and the diverse potential of students.

## 1. Emancipation and National Ideology Phase (1947-1968)

In the first two decades of independence, the main priority of national education was not high-level academic achievement. The government focused on dismantling the colonial mentality left by the Dutch and Japanese to build the character of independent, sovereign people.

### Rentjana Pelajaran 1947

The first curriculum of the Republic of Indonesia was drafted in 1947 and only became effective in schools in 1950. The term curriculum was not yet used; the government used the term *Rentjana Pelajaran* (Lesson Plan) to replace the Dutch *leerplan* system.

Its main focus was educating character, fostering state awareness, and building national independence. This curriculum reduced the portion of teaching that relied on intellectual memorization, replacing it with material directly connected to daily public events and regional arts.

### Rentjana Pelajaran Terurai 1952

Five years later, the government refined the first curriculum to make it more detailed. In the 1952 Detailed Lesson Plan (*Rentjana Pelajaran Terurai 1952*), the syllabus for each subject began to be clearly mapped out.

An important rule born in this era stated that one teacher would only teach one subject according to their expertise. Teachers were also required to connect lesson materials with everyday reality so students could apply their knowledge directly in the community.

### Rentjana Pendidikan 1964

Toward the mid-1960s, the government drafted the 1964 Education Plan (*Rentjana Pendidikan 1964*), which focused on mastering academic knowledge and practical skills for elementary schools.

This curriculum introduced the **Pancawardhana** concept, an effort to develop five complete aspects of the student:
1. Moral and ethical character.
2. Cognitive intelligence.
3. Emotional and artistic sensitivity.
4. Practical and manual skills (*keprigelan*).
5. Physical health.

### 1968 Curriculum

The transition of power to the New Order sharply changed the political direction of education. The 1968 Curriculum replaced the Pancawardhana concept with a structure focused purely on fostering the Pancasila spirit, basic knowledge, and special skills.

Pedagogically, the subject matter in this era became theoretical and separate from practical field issues. The learning content aimed to strengthen the state's ideological resilience, reinforce religious morals, and prepare a workforce for national development.

## 2. Instructional and Learning Efficiency Phase (1975-1994)

Entering the New Order's economic development era, the government demanded uniform, measurable, and efficient educational management from the central to the regional levels.

### 1975 Curriculum: Objective-Based Approach (PPSI)

The 1975 Curriculum fundamentally changed how teachers designed lessons. It adopted the Management by Objective (MBO) concept, translated into the educational world as the **Instructional System Development Procedure (PPSI)**.

Through PPSI, highly specific objectives controlled every learning activity, from General Instructional Objectives (TIU) to Specific Instructional Objectives (TIK). This approach was heavily influenced by behaviorist learning theory, which measures learning success by directly observable changes in student behavior.

The real impact in the staff room was the obligation to draft detailed Lesson Units (Satpel) for every sub-topic. Teachers spent a lot of time and energy writing administrative details before even entering the classroom.

### 1984 Curriculum: The Active Learning (CBSA) Era

Realizing that the 1975 Curriculum made students too passive as mere information receivers, the government launched the 1984 Curriculum. This curriculum promoted a process skill approach, popularly known as **Active Student Learning (CBSA)**.

Conceptually, CBSA positioned students as learning subjects who had to observe, group, discuss, and report their own findings. Unfortunately, this good idea was not matched by the methodological readiness of most educators in the field.

In many schools, group discussions were physically noisy but lacked cognitive reasoning. The unpreparedness of some teachers to help with active learning often turned classroom activities into mechanical routines without deep concept mastery.

### 1994 Curriculum and 1999 Supplement

The 1994 Curriculum attempted to combine the strict instructional goals of the 1975 Curriculum with the active learning process of the 1984 Curriculum. The division of school time used a trimester system, splitting one academic year into three evaluation stages.

However, this curriculum drew harsh criticism from educational practitioners because its material load was overloaded. Students had to complete heavy national content alongside local content in a limited time. When the Reform wave rolled in, the government issued the 1999 Curriculum Supplement to adjust certain materials without overhauling the main structure.

## 3. Competency-Based and Decentralization Phase (2004-2013)

The end of New Order centralization opened the way for regional autonomy, including in the education sector. The curriculum shifted from content-based (completing textbook material) to competency-based (measurable competency achievement).

### Competency-Based Curriculum (KBK 2004)

The 2004 KBK marked a major shift in the history of curriculum development in Indonesia. The learning focus no longer asked what material the teacher had taught, but rather what competencies the students had mastered after learning.

Teachers began to learn about classroom-based assessment, work portfolios, and formulating learning outcome indicators. Although initially applied gradually across various schools, KBK laid an important foundation for the next decentralized curriculum.

### School-Based Curriculum (KTSP 2006)

Two years after KBK, the government established KTSP 2006, giving broad autonomy to schools. The National Education Standards Agency (BSNP) only set the basic framework in the form of Content Standards (SI) and Graduate Competency Standards (SKL).

Each educational institution had the authority to independently develop syllabuses, determine indicators, and arrange Lesson Plans (RPP) according to their regional potential. This is where the challenge lay: schools with trained teacher resources could arrange rich operational curricula, while schools lacking guidance tended to copy syllabus documents from other schools without real adaptation.

### 2013 Curriculum (K-13): Attitude Integration and Scientific Approach

The 2013 Curriculum arrived to integrate character building, knowledge, and skills holistically through the structure of Core Competencies (KI) and Basic Competencies (KD). At the elementary school level, teachers presented lessons in an integrative-thematic way so students could see the connection between disciplines as a whole.

K-13 required the application of a scientific approach through five learning stages: Observing, Questioning, Gathering information, Reasoning, and Communicating. To run this approach, teachers were encouraged to use various [types of innovative learning models](/blog/types-of-innovative-learning-models) such as Discovery Learning, Inquiry, Problem-Based Learning (PBL), and Project-Based Learning (PjBL).

The biggest obstacle to K-13 during its initial application lay in the complexity of assessment administration. Teachers had to fill out authentic assessment rubric matrices for spiritual attitudes, social attitudes, knowledge, and skills simultaneously with dozens of KD items, before the government finally simplified that burden through curriculum revisions.

## 4. Flexibility and Deep Learning Phase: Merdeka Curriculum

The learning loss crisis pushed the government to simplify the material load so teachers could focus on the most basic competencies. This simplification effort gave birth to the **Merdeka Curriculum** (Independent Curriculum).

Through the Ministry of Education Regulation Number 12 of 2024 enacted in March 2024, the government officially established the Merdeka Curriculum as the basic framework and national curriculum structure for early childhood, primary, and secondary education. Schools still using the 2013 Curriculum have a transition period until the 2026/2027 academic year at the latest, and no later than 2027/2028 for schools in underdeveloped, frontier, and outermost regions (3T).

The Merdeka Curriculum is not an idea that appeared out of nowhere. It improves upon past weaknesses: taking the active student spirit from the 1984 Curriculum, reviving school autonomy like the 2006 KTSP, maintaining character strengthening from K-13, and then trimming material density so as not to repeat the overloaded content burden of the 1994 Curriculum.

The current policy direction within the Merdeka Curriculum framework also increasingly emphasizes strengthening literacy, numeracy, and a Deep Learning approach. This approach relies on three pillars of learning experience: Mindful Learning, Meaningful Learning (contextual learning), and Joyful Learning. For educators who want to combine time efficiency with a lively classroom atmosphere, [applying Accelerated Learning in the Merdeka Curriculum](/blog/accelerated-learning-merdeka-curriculum) can be an alternative strategy highly aligned with these principles.

## Comparison Table of 11 Indonesian National Curricula (1947-Merdeka)

To help map the shifts over time, the following table summarizes the eleven milestones in the history of curriculum development in Indonesia, along with their focus and practical challenges in the field:

| Curriculum | Foundation & Main Focus | Pedagogical Approach | Real Challenges in the Field |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Rentjana Pelajaran 1947** | Educational decolonization, shaping independent human character | Simple contextual learning based on daily life and arts | Limited school facilities and post-war teaching staff |
| **Rentjana Pelajaran Terurai 1952** | Detailed subject syllabus, close connection to public life | One teacher teaches one subject, focusing on practical material | Lack of uniform learning resource books in the regions |
| **Rentjana Pendidikan 1964** | Pancawardhana (moral, intelligence, emotional/artistic, practical skills, physical) | Developing creativity, feeling, intention, and practical works | National political dynamics affecting school stability |
| **1968 Curriculum** | Fostering pure Pancasila spirit, basic knowledge, special skills | Separated subject approach that was theoretical | Teaching material tended to be theoretical and rarely touched real issues |
| **1975 Curriculum** | Objective-oriented instructional efficiency (Management by Objective) | PPSI system with detailed general and specific goals (behaviorist) | Teachers burdened by writing highly rigid Lesson Units (Satpel) |
| **1984 Curriculum** | Process skill approach | Active Student Learning (CBSA) / Active Learning | Teacher methodological understanding was uneven, leading to superficial classroom activities |
| **1994 Curriculum** | Combining goals (1975) and process (1984) with a trimester system | Dense national and local content | Student learning load was too heavy (overloaded content) |
| **KBK 2004** | Measurable competency achievement post-Reform era | Based on learning outcome competencies and portfolio assessment | Technical confusion for teachers when switching from material targets to competencies |
| **KTSP 2006** | Decentralization and curriculum autonomy at the school level | Teachers and schools independently draft syllabuses from national standards | Inequality in syllabus quality across schools in different regions |
| **2013 Curriculum (K-13)** | Integration of attitudes, knowledge, and skills (KI/KD) | Scientific Approach (5M) and integrated thematic learning in elementary schools | Complexity of authentic assessment administration across four competency aspects |
| **Merdeka Curriculum (2024)** | Essential materials, phase flexibility, and character strengthening | Differentiated learning, Learning Outcomes (CP), co-curriculars, and Deep Learning | Demands teacher sensitivity to perform diagnostic assessments and design differentiation |

## Concrete Steps to Apply the Curriculum Essence in the Classroom

Understanding curriculum history should not stop at chronological memorization. As classroom practitioners, we need to translate the common thread of this curriculum evolution into daily lesson design.

::steps
### Break Down Learning Outcomes (CP) into Essential Goals

Unlike K-13, which set dozens of Basic Competencies (KD) per year, the Merdeka Curriculum provides Learning Outcomes (CP) per phase. Break down these outcomes into Learning Objectives (TP) and Learning Objective Flows (ATP) that focus on mastering essential concepts, rather than just rushing to finish textbook pages. Use operational verbs based on Bloom's Taxonomy that target Higher Order Thinking Skills.

### Conduct Diagnostic Assessments and Differentiated Learning

Do not jump straight into teaching with a single uniform method for all students who have different readiness levels. Conduct simple cognitive and non-cognitive diagnostic assessments at the start of a topic to map student learning readiness. From this mapping, apply differentiated learning: adjust the reading complexity (content differentiation), provide gradual help or scaffolding according to Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development (process differentiation), and give students the freedom to present their work as infographics, essays, audio recordings, or project prototypes (product differentiation).

### Activate Constructivism Through Contextual Learning Models

Avoid repeating the mistakes of the 1984 CBSA, which only made students physically busy without analytical thinking. Use learning models that require students to construct their own understanding by solving real problems. You can apply the [Problem Based Learning (PBL) syntax](/blog/problem-based-learning-stages-and-syntax) to train students' critical thinking on issues around the school, or guide them in designing collaborative work through structured PjBL stages.

### Increase the Portion of Reflective Formative Assessment

Shift the evaluation focus from final exams (summative) to ongoing process assessments (assessment as learning and assessment for learning). Use directed observation, regular feedback, and reflection sessions at the end of the lesson to make sure students truly understand concepts meaningfully, rather than just memorizing them temporarily.
::

## Common Pitfalls When Adopting a New Curriculum

When observing curriculum transitions in schools, three classic mistakes often repeat and hinder real change in the classroom.

First, **a document-dressing mentality**. Many educators are busy changing the cover of Lesson Plans to Teaching Modules and changing the term KD to CP, but when the classroom door closes, their teaching method remains a one-way lecture exactly like the 1975 era. True curriculum transformation happens in the dialogic interaction between teachers and students, not in the thickness of administrative documents.

Second, **cognitively poor group activities**. When applying active learning, teachers often feel successful just because the class looks busy discussing. In reality, without trigger questions based on Bloom's Taxonomy and observation sheets that challenge critical reasoning, group discussions will only become shallow mechanical routines.

Third, **treating co-curricular projects (P5) as high-cost exhibitions**. The essence of the Pancasila Student Profile Strengthening Project is the process of reflection, collaboration, and character habituation throughout the activity. Measuring the project's success solely by the grandeur of the exhibition or the final physical product strays from its pedagogical goals and financially burdens parents.

::warning
Avoid downloading ready-to-use teaching tools without modifying them. A good teaching module is not the thickest and most complex one, but the one that best suits your school's resources and the real conditions of the students in your class.
::

::faq
  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: How many times has Indonesia changed its curriculum since 1947?
  ---
  Indonesia has updated its national curriculum 11 times: the 1947 Lesson Plan, 1952 Detailed Lesson Plan, 1964 Education Plan, 1968 Curriculum, 1975 Curriculum, 1984 Curriculum, 1994 Curriculum (with 1999 Supplement), 2004 KBK, 2006 KTSP, 2013 Curriculum, and the Merdeka Curriculum.
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: What is the legal basis for establishing the Merdeka Curriculum as the national curriculum?
  ---
  The main legal foundation is the Ministry of Education Regulation Number 12 of 2024 concerning the Curriculum for Early Childhood Education, Primary Education, and Secondary Education, enacted in March 2024. This regulation officializes the Merdeka Curriculum as the national curriculum with a gradual transition period until the 2026/2027 academic year (and 2027/2028 for 3T regions).
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: What is the most fundamental difference between the 2013 Curriculum and the Merdeka Curriculum?
  ---
  The main difference lies in the flexibility of lesson hour allocation calculated annually, a focus on essential materials rather than target content completion, an evaluation system prioritizing diagnostic and formative assessments, and separate dedicated time for co-curricular character-strengthening projects.
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: Does the Merdeka Curriculum abolish the Science and Social Studies tracks in high school?
  ---
  Yes. At the high school Phase F level (grades 11 and 12), the rigid tracking system is abolished. Instead, students choose elective subjects tailored to their interests, talents, and future study or career plans.
  :::

::conclusion
Tracing the history of curriculum development in Indonesia makes us realize that even the best curriculum will only remain a stack of papers without proper execution in the classroom.

As a concrete step starting this week, take one of your lesson plans, cut the material targets that simply demand surface-level memorization, and replace them with one open-ended trigger question that prompts students to discuss and reason deeply. True freedom of learning begins when teachers dare to prioritize students' complete understanding over rushing through textbook pages.
