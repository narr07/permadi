The **cooperative learning model** is a structured instructional approach that places students into small, heterogeneous groups of four to five to complete academic tasks together. This approach combines individual responsibility with positive interdependence, meaning the success of one member directly affects the success of the entire team.

Many teachers assume that asking students to sit in a circle and complete a single worksheet together counts as cooperative learning. In reality, standard group work without clear structure almost always creates imbalance: one diligent student does all the work while the rest chat or simply put their names on the paper. As a fellow educator, I often see this happen when group instructions lack built-in accountability.

Syifa Siti Mukrimah explains in her book *53 Metode Belajar dan Pembelajaran* that group work only qualifies as cooperative if every child carries a measurable learning load and relies on others to reach the goal. This article breaks down how to design lively group activities, covering the five essential elements, the six-phase classroom syntax, and how to choose the right type for your teaching modules.

## What Is the Cooperative Learning Model and Its Main Focus?

In practical terms, the **cooperative learning model** shifts classroom interaction from individual competition to team collaboration. Students no longer race to finish first on their own; instead, they take responsibility for ensuring their groupmates also understand the material.

![Visual comparison between isolated group work and structured cooperative learning](/article/edu-cooperative-learning-ilustrasi.webp)

Sri Rahayu and Nofriansyah outline in *Model-Model Pembelajaran Abad 21* that this approach relies on peer interaction, individual accountability, and shared goals. Rooted in Lev Vygotsky's social constructivism theory, this method is widely recognized in international pedagogy.

Why do children often grasp explanations from peers more easily than lectures from teachers? The answer lies in Vygotsky's concepts of the Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) and scaffolding. A peer who already understands the topic acts as a cognitive bridge (the more knowledgeable other), translating complex concepts into everyday language.

In the Merdeka Curriculum, studies by Farman et al. (2024) and Gufron and Suryahadikusumah (2024) position cooperative learning as the primary path to achieving Deep Learning. This approach relies on mindful, meaningful, and joyful learning experiences. Children do not just memorize at the surface level; they build shared meaning while sharpening their collaboration and critical thinking skills.

To help you distinguish between traditional group work and true cooperative structures, look at this comparison:

| Comparison Aspect | Standard Group Work | Cooperative Learning Model |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Interdependence** | None; students can complete tasks separately. | Positive interdependence; tasks remain incomplete without everyone's input. |
| **Accountability** | Only one group grade; prone to free riders. | Grades come from individual quizzes accumulated into team points. |
| **Group Composition** | Often chosen freely by students (homogeneous based on friendships). | Assigned by the teacher heterogeneously (academics, gender, background). |
| **Leadership** | One appointed leader who tends to dominate. | Shared leadership through micro roles (note-taker, timekeeper, presenter). |
| **Social Skills** | Assumed to be automatically possessed by students. | Taught directly and evaluated at the end of the session. |

## 5 Essential Elements to Prevent Group Work from Burdening One Student

How do you transform standard group work into a complete **cooperative learning model**? Two pioneers of this approach, David W. Johnson and Roger T. Johnson, formulated five essential elements that must be present in every activity design. If even one of these elements is missing, your group activity risks reverting to standard group work.

### 1. Positive Interdependence
Students must realize that they "sink or swim together." Teachers design tasks so that no single child can reach the target alone. You can create this interdependence by dividing information sources (each child holds a different reading segment) or assigning interlocking work roles.

### 2. Individual Accountability
This element is the main antidote to the free-rider effect. Every group member must master the material completely because, at the end of the session, they will take an independent quiz without help from friends. Besides quizzes, teachers can also call on a random member to explain their team's work in front of the class.

### 3. Face-to-Face Promotive Interaction
Classroom desks are arranged face-to-face so students can easily exchange ideas, correct each other's understanding, and offer direct encouragement. This interaction is not just sitting close together while working on separate worksheets; it is an active dialogue to solve problems.

### 4. Interpersonal and Social Skills
Placing children who lack communication skills into a group and telling them to work together is a recipe for classroom chaos. Teachers must intentionally teach skills like active listening, politely disagreeing, taking turns to speak, and resolving conflicts.

### 5. Group Processing
Before the lesson ends, give each team dedicated time to reflect on how they worked. They evaluate which behaviors helped the discussion flow smoothly and which attitudes need improvement for the next meeting.

## Cooperative Learning Model Syntax (6 Standard Phases)

When you prepare lesson plans or [Merdeka teaching modules](/blog/merdeka-teaching-module-guide), you need a clear sequence of steps from the start to the end of the class. Referring to the guidelines in Syifa Siti Mukrimah's book *53 Metode Belajar dan Pembelajaran*, here are the six standard phases that form the basic framework of cooperative learning.

::steps
### Phase 1: Present Goals and Motivate Students
The teacher opens the class by presenting the learning objectives and explaining why the day's material matters to their lives. At this stage, spark student curiosity through trigger questions related to their daily routines.

### Phase 2: Present Information
Before students start discussing, the teacher presents the basic concepts briefly. You can give a short demonstration in front of the class, play a short video, or distribute introductory reading texts so all students share the same foundational knowledge.

### Phase 3: Organize Students into Learning Groups
The teacher divides students into small, heterogeneous groups of 4 to 5 people. Ensure students move their chairs in an orderly manner and that each group member immediately receives their specific work role.

### Phase 4: Guide Group Work and Study
While the discussion takes place, the teacher walks around the classroom observing teamwork and providing gradual help (scaffolding) for groups that hit a dead end. Your role here is to help with guiding questions, not to take over the discussion or give the answer key directly.

### Phase 5: Evaluation
The teacher tests material mastery through group presentations or individual quizzes. During the quiz, chairs are spaced out again, and students can no longer help each other so that each child's true mastery becomes clear.

### Phase 6: Provide Recognition
Based on individual evaluation results, the teacher calculates team scores and rewards groups that meet the success criteria. Rewards do not have to be expensive items; a star badge on the class board or a special round of applause means a lot to children.
::

## Types of Cooperative Learning and Classroom Application

Robert Slavin from Johns Hopkins University divides cooperative learning variations into two main umbrellas. The first is Structured Team Learning, which focuses on reward systems based on individual improvement scores, such as STAD and TGT. The second is Informal/Specialized Group Learning, which relies on task differentiation and group investigation dynamics, such as Jigsaw, Group Investigation (GI), and Think-Pair-Share (TPS).

Here are three of the most practical types you can apply directly in your classroom.

### 1. Jigsaw Type
If you are dealing with material that has several parallel and dense subchapters, the Jigsaw type is the most effective choice. As explained by Sri Rahayu and Nofriansyah in *Model-Model Pembelajaran Abad 21*, this type divides students into two dynamic groups: the Home Group and the Expert Group.

For example, in a primary school science and social studies class (Phase B or C) covering the topic "Ecosystems," the application flow goes like this:

1. **Forming Home Groups:** Divide the class into groups of 4. Number each member in the group from 1 to 4.
2. **Dividing Subtopics:** Break the Ecosystem material into four parts: (1) Producers, (2) Primary Consumers, (3) Apex Predators, and (4) Decomposers. Student number 1 gets the Producers subtopic, number 2 gets Primary Consumers, and so on.
3. **Expert Group Meeting:** All students with the same number from each home group gather at a new table. Number 1 students gather with other number 1s to dissect the Producers material completely using an expert guide sheet.
4. **Return to Home Groups:** After mastering their section, these "experts" return to their respective home groups. Taking turns, they teach their specialized subtopic to their three friends until all members understand the four ecosystem components entirely.
5. **Independent Quiz:** All students take a short test covering all four subtopics independently.

### 2. Student Teams Achievement Division (STAD)
Moch. Agus Krisno Budiyanto mentions in *Sintaks 45 Metode Pembelajaran dalam SCL* that STAD is the most straightforward cooperative type for beginners. The hallmark of STAD lies in how it calculates individual improvement points.

The uniqueness of Slavin's scoring system lies in its pedagogical fairness. In STAD, the points a child contributes to their group are not calculated from the absolute quiz score, but from how far that day's quiz score exceeds their baseline score.

For instance, a student whose score increases from 50 to 70 on today's quiz will contribute the same or more team points than a student who stays at 90. This growth-based grading system removes academic discrimination in the classroom: children who still struggle academically are no longer seen as burdens by their groupmates, but as teammates who can contribute high points if helped to study together.

### 3. Think-Pair-Share (TPS)
Do you lack a full two-hour lesson to rearrange classroom desks? You can rely on the Think-Pair-Share type. As stated in the *Panduan Guru Seni Tari untuk SD/MI Kelas V* (Revised Edition), this quick three-step method (Think → Pair → Share) is very flexible to insert in the middle of any lesson:

* **Think:** The teacher throws out a trigger question or a short case study, then gives a moment of silence for students to think of their own answers.
* **Pair:** Students turn to their desk mates to discuss their respective answers and formulate a shared conclusion.
* **Share:** The teacher calls on several pairs to share the results of their chat with the whole class.

| Cooperative Type | Material Characteristics | Main Activity Focus | Classroom Advantages |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Jigsaw** | Broad material divided into several equal subtopics (e.g., Science, History). | Rotation between Home Groups and Expert Groups. | Trains high responsibility because each student becomes the sole source of material for their team. |
| **STAD** | Material with definite answers or measurable procedures (e.g., Math, Grammar). | Guided practice in teams and individual improvement quizzes. | Motivates students with lower baseline skills through the individual improvement point system. |
| **Think-Pair-Share** | Trigger questions, art appreciation, or brief concept reflection. | Independent thinking followed by discussion with a desk mate. | Very fast preparation without needing to move classroom desks. |

## Integrating Cooperative and Differentiated Learning

Many fellow teachers ask how to combine the **cooperative learning model** with [differentiated instruction](/blog/differentiated-instruction-guide) in the Merdeka Curriculum. The two actually strengthen each other if you adjust the content and process.

When using the Jigsaw type, you can apply content differentiation subtly without making students feel singled out. Prepare four reading subtopics with varying text readability levels. You can direct students whose reading skills are still developing to handle subtopics equipped with many visual illustrations and short sentences, while advanced readers get subtopics with more detailed analytical text.

For process differentiation, divide micro roles within the home group. Children who like to move around can act as prop coordinators or scribes, while detail-oriented children can take the role of group understanding checkers.

::tip
The key to success for expert groups in the Jigsaw type is using a structured expert sheet. Before you allow students to return to their home groups, check their answers in the expert group first. Ensure no misconceptions are carried back to their home groups.
::

## 4 Common Mistakes When Applying Cooperative Learning

Managing a class full of children's discussions is indeed challenging. To prevent your class from turning noisy with no learning outcomes, avoid these four traps that educators experience most often.

### 1. Letting Students Choose Their Own Groups
When teachers let students choose their own groups, the result is almost certainly homogeneous groups based on friendship cliques. Smart children will gather with other smart children, while quiet children will be left out in the corner of the class.

Sri Rahayu and Nofriansyah in *Model-Model Pembelajaran Abad 21* emphasize that teachers must arrange group members based on initial diagnostic assessment data. Mix students' academic ability levels, learning styles, and personalities so they get used to helping each other in a team.

### 2. Equating Group Seating with Cooperative Learning
Combining four desks into one large island does not automatically make students collaborate. If the task you assign is still a standard worksheet that can be done individually, they will just sit close together while working alone. Design step-by-step, interlocking worksheets; for example, information in student A's section is needed to complete student B's section.

### 3. Ignoring Individual Quizzes and Micro Roles
When teachers only take one grade from the final group product, passive students will hand over the entire task burden to their diligent friends. Prevent this by always holding a [formative assessment](/blog/formative-assessment-in-classroom) in the form of an independent quiz at the end of the meeting. Also, require each group to divide micro roles from the start of the discussion: who acts as the note-taker, timekeeper, understanding checker, and spokesperson.

### 4. Skipping Team Evaluation (Group Processing)
Because they are too absorbed in discussion, class time often runs out before the group processing evaluation stage happens. However, setting aside 5 minutes at the end of the session to answer the questions "What did our group do well today?" and "What is one thing we need to improve for the next discussion?" helps build children's social maturity.

::warning
Watch out for symptoms of social fatigue in students. Not all activities are suitable for group work. Keep the class rhythm balanced between collaborative problem-solving activities and quiet time for mindful reflection.
::

::faq
  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: What is the fundamental difference between standard group work and the cooperative learning model?
  ---
  Standard group work generally only requires one shared task to be completed without binding role divisions and individual responsibilities, making it prone to domination by a few students. Conversely, the cooperative learning model binds students with positive interdependence, clear micro roles, and dual grading that combines individual quiz results and group performance.
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: What are individual improvement scores in the STAD type and how are they calculated?
  ---
  Individual improvement scores are points given to students based on how far their latest quiz score exceeds their own baseline score, rather than the size of the absolute score. This method gives a fair chance for students with lower baseline skills to contribute maximum points to their group as long as they show learning improvement.
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: Why does the Jigsaw type often trigger confusion in class and how do you overcome it?
  ---
  The Jigsaw type usually triggers confusion because students in the expert group have not truly mastered their subtopic before returning to the home group. To overcome this, provide an expert sheet containing key points they must dissect, then ensure each expert group understands the material directly before they return to the home group.
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: How do you integrate cooperative learning with differentiated instruction?
  ---
  You can combine them through content and process differentiation. For content differentiation, adjust the text readability level for each subtopic in the Jigsaw type according to students' learning readiness. For process differentiation, assign work roles within the group (such as spokesperson, understanding checker, or note-taker) that align with students' strengths and interests.
  :::

::conclusion
The **cooperative learning model** is not just a variation in arranging classroom desks; it is a measurable strategy to train students' reasoning sharpness and social sensitivity. To start this week, you do not need to overhaul your entire teaching schedule immediately. Try applying the Think-Pair-Share type in your next meeting to get children used to guided discussions. Once students are accustomed to listening and respecting their friends' opinions, gradually upgrade to the STAD or Jigsaw type using your classroom diagnostic data.
