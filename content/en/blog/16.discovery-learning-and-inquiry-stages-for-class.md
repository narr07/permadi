The syntax of discovery learning and inquiry in the classroom represents a systematic sequence of phases that guide students to discover concepts inductively or investigate problems through the scientific method. Discovery learning has six stages from stimulation to generalization to uncover standard concepts, while inquiry learning moves from orientation to hypothesis testing to prove empirical truth.

Asking students to memorize the definition of photosynthesis or the surface area formula of a 3D shape often only leads to a shallow understanding on test papers. When faced with natural phenomena or modified questions, they fail to connect field facts with textbook theories. The human brain learns most effectively when discovering patterns independently.

The Merdeka Curriculum paradigm encourages a deep learning approach that is meaningful, mindful, and joyful. The syntax of learning models is no longer just an administrative supplement in teaching modules. This sequence of steps works as a thinking framework to train students' critical thinking and scientific reasoning while pursuing Learning Outcomes.

If you want to map the position of these two models among various other pedagogical frameworks, review the guide on [Types of Innovative Learning Models and Syntax](/blog/types-of-innovative-learning-models) as a compass for planning your teaching.

## Fundamental Differences Between Discovery Learning and Inquiry Learning

Many educators equate these two models because both require students to actively seek information. However, their philosophical foundations and learning endpoints differ.

Jerome Bruner initiated discovery learning from cognitive-constructivist psychology roots. Its main focus is to guide students in organizing learning materials until they discover concepts, principles, laws, or relationship patterns that are actually already scientifically defined. Teachers already know the conceptual endpoint students must reach, but deliberately hide it behind a series of data or manipulative objects.

In contrast, John Dewey and Richard Suchman developed inquiry learning by imitating how scientists work in laboratories and in the field. Students do not just look for existing formulas or definitions. They act like researchers who identify empirical puzzles, formulate hypotheses, design experiments, collect field evidence, and test those hypotheses to draw valid conclusions.

Notice the comparison between these two learning models in the following table:

| Comparison Aspect | Discovery Learning | Inquiry Learning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Theoretical Pioneer** | Jerome Bruner | John Dewey, Richard Suchman |
| **Main Focus** | Discovering standard concepts, formulas, or principles through directed exploration. | Solving problems or answering scientific questions by testing hypotheses. |
| **Reasoning Direction** | Dominantly inductive (from specific observations to general rules). | Deductive-inductive cycle (formulating hypotheses and testing them with empirical data). |
| **Process Direction** | Tends to be guided (guided discovery); the concept endpoint is certain. | Ranges from structured to fully independent (open inquiry); findings may vary. |
| **Teacher's Role** | Providing data or trigger objects and keeping students on the concept track. | Facilitating research methodology, testing variable validity, and challenging student arguments. |
| **Ideal Material Example** | Discovering the cylinder area formula, language rules, or Archimedes' principle. | Investigating the causes of village well water pollution or object weathering factors. |

::note
Never reveal the final concept conclusion or formula at the beginning of the Student Worksheet. Providing a definition before the data induction session ends will kill curiosity and ruin the whole essence of independent discovery.
::

## Six Steps of Discovery Learning and Inquiry Syntax in the Classroom

Managing discovery or inquiry based classes requires strict phase sequencing. Teachers must not skip a single stage because each step acts as a cognitive foundation for the next.

### Standard Sequence of Discovery Learning Syntax

Referring to educational process standards and Bruner's cognitive framework, here are the six stages that teachers must execute in class:

::steps
### Stimulation
Teachers start the class by showing anomalous phenomena, demonstrating short events that provoke questions, or distributing concrete objects without theoretical explanations. This step aims to create cognitive conflict so students feel prompted to ask questions.

### Problem Statement
Students formulate specific questions from the confusion that arises during the stimulation stage. The teacher helps students select one or two key questions relevant to the Learning Objectives as working hypotheses.

### Data Collection
Students explore actively to find relevant information. They observe physical objects, measure dimensions, record simple trial results, or search through curated literature.

### Data Processing
Students classify, tabulate, or calculate all collected information and raw numbers. They look for relationship patterns between variables in tables to see emerging trends.

### Verification
Students cross check their findings carefully. They match patterns from the data processing stage with initial guesses while comparing them to valid theories in textbooks or reference sources.

### Generalization
Students formulate general principles, concept definitions, or mathematical formulas using their own words. The teacher closes this cycle by confirming these findings so the whole class understands the concept uniformly and accurately.
::

### Standard Sequence of Inquiry Learning Syntax

The inquiry model places hypothesis testing at the heart of learning. Adapting the inquiry learning framework popularly applied in Indonesian schools (such as those formulated by Wina Sanjaya and Richard Suchman), here are its six main phases:

::steps
### Problem Orientation
The teacher builds a responsive and conducive learning atmosphere. At this stage, the teacher explains the study topic, explains the investigation objectives, and clarifies the scientific work procedures students will follow during the session.

### Formulating the Problem
The teacher brings students to a scientific problem or puzzle that challenges reasoning. A good problem is an open question containing alternative answers that can be tested empirically in the field or laboratory.

### Formulating a Hypothesis
Students propose temporary answers or guesses to the defined problem. The teacher trains students to construct logical hypotheses that have a strong initial rationale and can be tested for truth through evidence collection.

### Collecting Data
Students design and run investigations to gather hypothesis testing information. This activity requires students to observe carefully, interview sources, control experimental variables, and record data honestly.

### Testing the Hypothesis
This critical phase determines whether the initial guess is accepted or rejected. Students analyze collected empirical data to assess the truth level of the hypothesis based on objective facts, not just assumptions or feelings.

### Formulating Conclusions
Students describe final findings based on hypothesis testing results. They present the proven cause-and-effect relationships along with supporting evidence to the class.
::

If the problem you raise in class focuses more on solving cross-disciplinary contextual case studies rather than proving basic concepts, you might consider [Problem Based Learning Stages and Practical Syntax](/blog/problem-based-learning-stages-and-syntax) as an alternative teaching framework.

## Concrete Application Scenarios in the Classroom

Pedagogical theories often feel abstract until we translate them into real activities at the whiteboard. Here are practical scenario examples to help you manage time flow and student interactions when applying discovery learning and inquiry syntax in the classroom.

### Discovery Learning Example: Phase D (Middle School) Math

**Topic**: Discovering the Cylinder Surface Area Formula.

1. **Stimulation**: The teacher brings used milk cans to the front of the class and peels off the paper labels. The teacher throws a trigger question, "If a factory wants to print paper labels that cover the entire surface of this can, including the top and bottom without overlapping, how do they calculate the area of paper needed?"
2. **Problem Statement**: Students identify the problem into a focused question: "What 2D shapes make up the surface of a cylinder when disassembled, and how do we calculate the total area?"
3. **Data Collection**: In groups, students cut cardboard cylinder replicas along the edges until they unfold into cylinder nets. They find two identical circles and one rectangle (the cylinder blanket), then measure the circle's radius and the rectangle's length and width.
4. **Data Processing**: Students record measurements into their worksheets. With teacher guidance, they analyze a unique relationship: the long side of the blanket rectangle exactly surrounds the base circle's edge. Students write an equation stating the rectangle's length equals the circle's circumference ($2\pi r$), while its width equals the cylinder's height ($h$).
5. **Verification**: Students calculate the net's surface area using manual multiplication, then test it on a second can of a different size. They verify the formula $2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rh$ with explanations in math textbooks.
6. **Generalization**: Students conclude the general formula for a cylinder's surface area in front of the class, which is $A = 2\pi r(r + h)$.

### Inquiry Learning Example: Phase C (Elementary) / Phase D (Middle School) Science

**Topic**: Investigating Water Quality in the School Environment.

1. **Orientation**: The teacher shows two water samples from a well near a rice field and a well in a densely populated area far from fields.
2. **Formulating the Problem**: Students observe the differences between the two samples and formulate a problem: "Why do plants watered with well water near the rice field grow faster, but the water gets mossy quicker compared to residential well water?"
3. **Formulating a Hypothesis**: Students discuss and propose a hypothesis: "Agricultural fertilizer residues (nitrates or phosphates) seeping into the rice field well water trigger algae and vegetation growth."
4. **Collecting Data**: Students divide tasks in investigation teams. They test simple indicators using a pH meter, test clarity, note sediments, and interview local farmers regarding fertilizer use in the fields.
5. **Testing the Hypothesis**: Students compare acidity levels, dissolved particles, and farmer interview results with science literature on agricultural fertilizer pollution. Field evidence confirms that fertilizer nutrient runoff triggers moss growth.
6. **Formulating Conclusions**: Students conclude that agricultural fertilizer runoff affects well water quality parameters, then compile suggestions for local environmental management.

## Scaffolding Strategy, Cognitive Load, and Inquiry Differentiation

Many teachers complain that discovery or inquiry methods make classes noisy, take too much time, and confuse students. This failure generally happens because teachers let students explore without guidance (unguided discovery).

A meta-analysis by **Alfieri, Aldrich, Brooks, and Tenenbaum (2011)** in the *Journal of Educational Psychology* proves an important finding. They concluded that unguided discovery is ineffective and often triggers misconceptions and cognitive frustration. Conversely, **guided discovery** and scaffolded inquiry equipped with structured feedback produce a high positive impact on concept understanding and long-term memory retention.

### Managing Cognitive Load (Cognitive Load Theory)

Why does unguided discovery often fail in class? The answer lies in the Cognitive Load Theory formulated by John Sweller. Human working memory has limited capacity when processing new information.

When you ask students to find new concepts while requiring them to design their own tables, operate measuring tools they haven't mastered, and guess ambiguous work instructions, their working memory experiences cognitive overload. The mental load exhausted on dealing with technical complexities is called extraneous cognitive load. As a result, there is no memory space left for germane cognitive load, which is the actual mental process of building concept understanding schemas during the data processing and generalization stages.

Reduce this unnecessary cognitive load by preparing worksheets with clean observation table frameworks. Briefly demonstrate how to use props at the start of the session so students' mental energy is fully focused on analyzing data patterns.

### Process Differentiation with Four Levels of Inquiry

In a heterogeneous classroom, student readiness is never uniform. Forcing all students to do open inquiry when most do not yet understand how to control experimental variables will only mess up classroom management.

You can differentiate the learning process by adapting the four-level spectrum of inquiry from Herron, as well as Banchi and Bell:

| Level of Inquiry | Problem Question | Investigation Procedure | Analysis & Conclusion | Target Student Readiness |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Confirmation Inquiry** | Provided by teacher | Provided by teacher | Already known (concept verification) | Students who need a basic introduction to scientific work methods. |
| **2. Structured Inquiry** | Provided by teacher | Provided by teacher | Formulated independently by students | Students who still need step-by-step work structures. |
| **3. Guided Inquiry** | Provided by teacher | Designed independently by students | Formulated independently by students | Students with intermediate independence ready to design experiments. |
| **4. Open Inquiry** | Formulated by students | Designed independently by students | Formulated independently by students | Advanced students ready to conduct independent science research projects. |

Through the matrix above, you can distribute different worksheet versions within the same lesson. Give the Structured Inquiry worksheet with detailed procedures to groups still struggling to read data. Meanwhile, give the Guided Inquiry worksheet containing only the problem question to groups already skilled in critical reasoning.

## Common Mistakes When Executing Syntax and Their Solutions

Applying discovery learning and inquiry syntax in the classroom requires high sensitivity in reading student learning dynamics. Here are four traps most often experienced by educators along with steps to overcome them:

1. **Missing the Essence of the Stimulation Stage**: Many teachers immediately hand out worksheets and tell students to work on questions without providing initial stimulation. Without curiosity or cognitive conflict in the first minute, students just view the worksheet as a boring routine task. Start the class by showing a mini-demonstration or an anomaly video that provokes curiosity, because this curiosity triggers dopamine release that helps consolidate memory in the hippocampus when students reach the conclusion stage.
2. **Mixing Up Data Processing and Verification**: Students often jump straight from calculation tables (data processing) to drawing conclusions (generalization) without going through verification. Consequently, if their measurement data is wrong due to tool errors, the conclusions they draw will also stray and create misconceptions. Require students to align their group findings with textbooks or reliable reference sources during the verification stage.
3. **Losing Control of Time Management**: The data collection stage is highly prone to sucking up the entire lesson hour because students get too absorbed in trying out props. Apply strict time-boxing techniques. Set a countdown timer on the whiteboard for each syntax step, for example, limiting data collection to 15 minutes.
4. **Only Grading the Final Result on Test Papers**: Running a long discovery process but only taking grades from multiple-choice questions at the end of the chapter is an educational irony. Use authentic formative assessments in the form of process rubrics to assess students' skills in observing, formulating hypotheses, processing data carefully, and collaborating in teams.

::warning
Avoid the habit of immediately blaming students with phrases like "Your group's conclusion is wrong" during the presentation phase. Use Socratic questioning techniques during the verification stage to guide students in reviewing their data anomalies until they realize their logical errors themselves.
::

::tip
Ask representatives from two groups that obtained different data results to present their findings side by side. Differences in observation results between groups will actually spark lively scientific discussions in class.
::

::faq
  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: When should teachers choose Discovery Learning over Inquiry Learning?
  ---
  Choose Discovery Learning when your learning target is guiding students to understand concept definitions, basic formulas, or fixed rules (like basic physics laws, language rules, and geometry formulas). Choose Inquiry Learning when the goal is training research skills, scientific investigation, and testing guesses on open problems.
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: Is Discovery Learning syntax suitable for all grade levels?
  ---
  It is highly suitable, provided it is adjusted to Piaget's cognitive development levels and Bruner's scaffolding. The Foundation Phase and Phase A use concrete object manipulation (enactive), Phases B through D use images or diagrams (iconic), while Phases E and F are capable of reasoning with abstract or symbolic formulas (symbolic).
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: How do you integrate Discovery and Inquiry syntax into Merdeka Curriculum Teaching Modules?
  ---
  Insert all six syntax phases sequentially into the Core Activities subsection in the Teaching Module. Combine each syntax stage with Learning Objectives, continuous formative assessments, and both process and content differentiation.
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: What if student conclusions are wrong during the Generalization stage?
  ---
  Teachers must not immediately blame them, but rather guide students with rhetorical questions (Socratic questioning) during the Verification stage. Direct them to recheck the data processing table and match it with reference literature so they find their own mistakes.
  :::
::

::conclusion
Mastering discovery learning and inquiry syntax in the classroom gives you full control to transform a passive learning space into an active thinking laboratory. As an action guide before you compile your next Teaching Module:

- **Use Discovery Learning** to dissect essential concepts and basic formulas through six steps: Stimulation, Problem Statement, Data Collection, Data Processing, Verification, and Generalization.
- **Use Inquiry Learning** to train scientific reasoning through the cycle: Orientation, Formulating the Problem, Formulating a Hypothesis, Collecting Data, Testing the Hypothesis, and Formulating Conclusions.
- **Prepare Tiered Worksheets** with clean tabulation formats to suppress extraneous cognitive load and adjust the inquiry freedom level to your students' learning readiness.
