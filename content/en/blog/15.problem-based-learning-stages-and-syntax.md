**Applying Problem Based Learning and PBL syntax** in elementary schools is a teaching strategy that uses real-world problems around the school as a starting point for learning. Through Richard I. Arends' five phases of work, students observe open-ended problems, organize teams, investigate field data, present solution ideas, and evaluate their thinking process independently and collaboratively.

Giving a geometric area formula or ecosystem definition at the start of a lesson and then asking students to complete ten practice questions will not build reasoning skills. This conventional pattern only creates procedural compliance. The moment students face real-world problems outside their textbooks, they get confused because they cannot find a ready-to-use formula.

The Problem Based Learning (PBL) approach completely reverses this flow. Teachers present an unsolved contextual puzzle in the first minute before introducing any theory. Students then learn scientific concepts because they need them to solve the problem right in front of them.

## Summary of 5 PBL Syntax Phases and the Deep Learning Context

Before looking at case studies for each subject, we need to align our understanding of how this model works in the classroom. The standard reference in the Merdeka Curriculum points to the five instructional stages by Richard I. Arends.

Here is a brief map dividing the roles of teachers and students throughout the **PBL syntax**:

| Phase | Phase Name (Arends) | Teacher Roles & Behaviors | Student Key Activities |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Phase 1** | Orienting students to the problem | Presenting an authentic problem (Meaningful), explaining learning goals, motivating students. | Observing real stimuli, formulating questions, identifying the root problem. |
| **Phase 2** | Organizing students to study | Helping group division, limiting the problem focus, guiding the division of tasks. | Forming teams, dividing exploration tasks, deciding on necessary tools and resources. |
| **Phase 3** | Guiding individual & group investigations | Providing facilities and scaffolding, monitoring data collection, sparking critical thinking (Joyful). | Doing experiments, interviews, or literature reviews, then recording findings on the worksheet. |
| **Phase 4** | Developing and presenting artifacts | Helping plan the solution report artifact, setting up a mechanism to share work in class. | Creating a poster, report, or solution sketch, then presenting arguments to the class. |
| **Phase 5** | Analyzing and evaluating the problem-solving process | Guiding metacognitive reflection (Mindful), evaluating solution effectiveness, and clarifying concepts. | Reflecting on teamwork steps, evaluating the strengths and weaknesses of other groups' solutions. |

If you need a breakdown of constructivist theory, pedagogical foundations, and prompt dialogue sentences for each stage in the table above, please read our specific review in the [Complete Guide to Problem Based Learning Syntax Steps](/blog/syntax-problem-based-learning). In this article, we will jump straight into classroom execution in elementary schools.

The five phase structure above naturally fits the Deep Learning approach championed by the Minister of Primary and Secondary Education (Mendikdasmen) Abdul Mu'ti through its three main pillars: **Mindful**, **Meaningful**, and **Joyful**. In line with the Learning Outcomes guidelines in BSKAP decree Number 032/H/KR/2024, the learning process shifts from memorizing materials to contextual reasoning and authentic assessment.

The *Meaningful* aspect appears in Phase 1 when students realize the problem discussed actually happens in their school environment. A *Joyful* atmosphere grows in Phase 3 and Phase 4 when students experiment directly outside their desks without the pressure of memorizing texts. Finally, *Mindful* awareness locks in during Phase 5 when students evaluate their own thinking process: why their group's solution worked or missed the initial expectation.

## Case Studies on Applying Problem Based Learning and PBL Syntax in Elementary Classes

The biggest challenge for elementary teachers when making Teaching Modules is translating abstract Learning Outcomes (CP) into problem scenarios close to a child's world. Many teachers fall into the trap of giving textbook word problems and claiming them as PBL.

The following three case studies are ready to use for elementary school Phase B and Phase C. Each plan includes an authentic, open-ended problem (ill-structured problem), a Teaching Module flow snippet, differentiation strategies, and authentic assessment rubric criteria.

### 1. Science and Social Studies Case Study (Phase C Grade 5): School Garden Ecosystem

In Science and Social Studies (IPAS), the schoolyard is the best laboratory. Avoid starting an ecosystem chapter by telling students to copy the definitions of biotic and abiotic components from the whiteboard.

**Authentic Problem Trigger (Phase 1):**
> "Water spinach and chili plants in the school's hydroponic garden or mini park have turned yellow and wilted over the past two weeks. However, the morning watering schedule is never missed. The gardener suspects a problem with water quality or sunlight exposure, but does not know the exact cause yet. How can we investigate what is making these plants wilt and restore the school garden?"

**Teaching Module Flow Snippet:**
* **Learning Objective (TP):** Students are able to analyze abiotic factors affecting plant growth and propose scientific solutions to improve the mini garden environment based on observation evidence.
* **Syntax Execution (Phase 1 to Phase 3):** The teacher takes students directly to observe the mini garden. Students check the physical yellowing leaves, measure water pH or check bed soil moisture, compare shaded areas with open areas, and record data on observation sheets.
* **Syntax Execution (Phase 4 and Phase 5):** Students return to class, match field data with reading materials about symptoms of light and nutrient deficiency, and formulate recommendations to save the garden.

**Differentiated Learning Strategies:**
* **Content Differentiation:** Groups ready to learn quickly read simple scientific articles and vegetable plant nutrition tables. Groups needing visual guidance study illustrated infographic posters about symptoms of nutrient or sunlight deficiency.
* **Process Differentiation:** The teacher gives structured investigation guide cards (step-by-step scaffolding) to groups needing extra assistance when determining the order of observation in the garden.
* **Product Differentiation:** Each team is free to choose how to present their solution in Phase 4, from an illustrated garden improvement flow poster, a short video recording explaining the solution, to a garden nutrition schedule recommendation table to pin on the class board.

**Authentic Assessment Rubric (Science & Social Studies):**

| Assessment Criteria | Needs Guidance (1) | Fair (2) | Good (3) | Excellent (4) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Abiotic Problem Identification** | Mentions wilting plant conditions without linking them to environmental factors. | Links wilting plant symptoms to 1 abiotic factor without data evidence. | Connects physical plant symptoms to at least 2 abiotic factors with observation notes. | Accurately analyzes the relationship between light, water, and planting media based on field observation data. |
| **Scientific Solution Validity** | The solution is unrelated to the root cause (just suggesting to replace the plants). | The solution makes sense but is hard for students to practice at school. | The solution is logical, realistic to do in the school garden, and answers the root cause. | The solution is highly applicable, based on observation evidence, and includes long-term prevention steps. |

### 2. Mathematics Case Study (Phase B Grade 4): Measuring the Reading Corner Area

In elementary Mathematics, **applying Problem Based Learning and PBL syntax** is very effective at removing children's fear of numbers. The main key is adding real constraints like limited class funds or space availability, so students cannot solve the problem with just one multiplication.

**Authentic Problem Trigger (Phase 1):**
> "Our class reading corner will be fitted with 30 cm x 30 cm puzzle foam mats and a small bookshelf. The remaining empty floor space is 2 meters long and 1.5 meters wide. However, our class fund is only enough to buy a maximum of 20 mat pieces. What is the best mat arrangement to fit 4 students sitting at once without blocking the classroom door?"

**Teaching Module Flow Snippet:**
* **Learning Objective (TP):** Students are able to estimate and calculate surface area using area units, and apply it in classroom spatial planning.
* **Syntax Execution (Phase 2 and Phase 3):** Groups take a measuring tape to measure the actual area in the corner of the class. Students calculate the area of one mat piece, calculate the total area of the 20 mat pieces they can buy, and test several alternative layout shapes on grid paper so the door can still open.
* **Syntax Execution (Phase 4 and Phase 5):** Groups pin their layout sketch drafts on the display board and prove to their peers why the arrangement is the most functional for four people.

**Differentiated Learning Strategies:**
* **Content Differentiation:** The teacher prepares 1:10 scale grid paper for visual or kinesthetic students, and plain paper for students with strong abstract reasoning.
* **Process Differentiation:** Students who still struggle to multiply surface areas use concrete physical unit boxes (manipulatives) to simulate mat pieces directly on their desks.
* **Product Differentiation:** Groups can choose to demonstrate the layout directly on the corner floor, or present a mini Reading Corner architectural sketch on the display board.

**Authentic Assessment Rubric (Mathematics):**

| Assessment Criteria | Needs Guidance (1) | Fair (2) | Good (3) | Excellent (4) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Calculation & Conversion Accuracy** | Makes errors converting meters to centimeters and miscalculates the number of pieces. | Size conversion is correct, but there are calculation errors when adding the total mat area. | Area calculations and the number of mat pieces (maximum 20 pieces) are correct without calculation errors. | Area calculations are highly accurate, complete with a comparison of the remaining floor space not covered by mats. |
| **Space Solution Feasibility** | Arrangement exceeds 20 pieces or blocks the classroom door path. | Uses a maximum of 20 pieces but is too narrow for 4 kids to sit. | Layout plan obeys the 20 piece limit, fits 4 students, and does not block the door. | Layout design is highly functional, obeys all budget and space limits, and is neat. |

### 3. Indonesian Language Case Study (Phase C Grade 6): Environmental Persuasion Text

Language lessons often lose their spark because students are told to write essays for fictional readers. Through PBL, teachers turn writing activities into a communication mission to improve the behavior of real people around the school.

**Authentic Problem Trigger (Phase 1):**
> "Many grade 1 to 3 students buy snacks outside the school fence during dismissal time and leave plastic waste in the drainage ditch in front of the gate. This habit triggers bad smells and puddles when it rains. The school has forbidden this through weekly verbal announcements, but they are ignored. As grade 6 seniors, how can we find out the reasons why they litter and design an appeal medium that younger students will actually follow?"

**Teaching Module Flow Snippet:**
* **Learning Objective (TP):** Students are able to draft effective persuasion texts based on interview data to persuade younger students to keep the school environment clean.
* **Syntax Execution (Phase 2 and Phase 3):** Group representatives interview the school janitor and several grade 1 to 3 students to find out why they throw trash in the ditch. Students note whether the problem is that the trash cans are too far, too tall, or if the younger students just do not know better.
* **Syntax Execution (Phase 4 and Phase 5):** Based on interview findings, groups design a persuasive message text that fits a young child's psychology (short sentences, colorful visuals, or rhymes), rather than a rigid prohibition sentence.

**Differentiated Learning Strategies:**
* **Content Differentiation:** As an initial data source in class, the teacher provides interview voice recordings, short text transcripts, and visual data in the form of photos of trash piles in the ditch.
* **Process Differentiation:** The teacher assists groups needing guidance to structure simple appeal sentences. Meanwhile, independent groups design persuasive messages using rhymes or children's poetry.
* **Product Differentiation:** The persuasion text does not have to be an essay. Students are free to create a 4-panel comic strip, an illustrated mini flyer, or a school radio broadcast script to read during recess.

**Authentic Assessment Rubric (Indonesian Language):**

| Assessment Criteria | Needs Guidance (1) | Fair (2) | Good (3) | Excellent (4) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Argument Structure & Data** | Sentences only contain prohibitions without explaining logical reasons. | Includes an appeal to keep clean but has not included interview findings. | Presents a clear cause-and-effect relationship (littering clogs drains) based on interview data. | Argument is very strong, integrating younger student interview findings into targeted solutions and appeals. |
| **Persuasive Power & Target Audience** | Language is too rigid like an office announcement and hard for lower grade kids to grasp. | Language is polite enough but fails to attract the attention of kids in grades 1 to 3. | Word choice is polite, engaging, and uses visual elements or rhymes fitting the younger students' age. | The message is highly captivating, empathetic, creative, and aligns with the psychological profile of younger kids. |

::warning
Do not confuse the product demands in Phase 4 above with creating long-term projects. Referring to the classic distinction by Barrows (1996) and Thomas (2000), PBL is oriented toward formulating solutions to problems (problem-resolution), so posters, reading corner sketches, or comic strips are just tools to communicate arguments over 1 to 2 meetings. If the main target of your Learning Outcome is to design and produce a large-scale physical work (end-product design), use the [Project Based Learning Guide and PjBL Syntax](/blog/project-based-learning-guide-and-syntax).
::

## Classroom Management Tactics When Running PBL Syntax

Designing a neat Teaching Module on paper only completes half the job. The other half depends on your flexibility in managing the dynamics of active, easily distracted elementary students with limited attention spans.

Here are three practical tactics in the classroom so running the **PBL syntax** does not end in aimless noise or running out of lesson time.

::steps
### Use a Tiered Problem Trigger Strategy in Phase 1
In one elementary class, student reading comprehension skills vary greatly. If you just hand out a single sheet of long problem narrative text, students who are not fluent readers will give up in the first minute. Present the same problem in 2 to 3 delivery complexity levels (tiered problem trigger) based on student readiness: show real photos or a 30-second video on the projector for the whole class, give illustrated case sheets with short sentences to groups needing guidance, and give a complete case sheet with extra data to groups ready for more challenge. With this strategy, you run differentiation without needing to make three separate lesson plans.

### Replace Direct Instructions with Prompt Questions in Phase 3
During investigations, elementary teachers often cannot stand seeing a quiet class or get anxious seeing students looking confused (low tolerance to struggle). As a result, teachers directly dictate the solution steps from start to finish. Resist the urge to tell them how to calculate or answer directly. Use scaffolding techniques through prompt questions whenever you walk around visiting group desks: ask "What do you already know from your observations?" and "What data is still missing for you to make a decision?"

### Replace One-by-One Presentations with Marketplace Activity in Phase 4
Asking six groups to come to the front of the class one by one will drain time and bore other students. When the first group speaks up front, the other five groups are busy chatting, and the lesson ends before you even reach Phase 5. Use the Marketplace Presentation or Gallery Walk technique: one member guards the group's poster booth to explain their solution, while other members walk around visiting friends' booths to listen and give feedback using star stickers. All groups move actively at the same time, and you can secure time for final reflection.
::

For those who want to see how this problem-based investigation model differs from pure concept discovery in the lab, please compare its flow with our review of [Discovery Learning and Inquiry Stages for Class](/blog/discovery-learning-and-inquiry-stages-for-class).

## Common PBL Application Mistakes and Practical Solutions

Many teachers feel reluctant to apply PBL because their first try feels exhausting and learning goals are unmet. If traced back, these failures almost always root in the following five operational traps:

| Common Classroom Mistake | Why Does This Happen? | Practical Solution for Teachers |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Ordinary math word problems are claimed as PBL** | The problem is closed with only one definite answer (not an ill-structured problem). | Make the problem have multiple solutions or demand trade-offs, like budget limits, space limits, or user preferences. |
| **Teachers give step-by-step instructions directly** | Teachers are afraid of silence or anxious seeing students confused (low tolerance to struggle). | Use prompt question scaffolding: ask "What do you already know?" and "What data is missing?", instead of telling them how to calculate it. |
| **Products are rated higher than the investigation process** | Teachers confuse PBL with PjBL, demanding complex artworks that take a lot of time. | State in the rubric that the PBL artifact is just a tool to explain solution logic (a one-page poster, flowchart, or recommendation sheet is enough). |
| **Differentiation is ignored with equal reading loads** | Teachers give a long problem narrative text without considering the diverse literacy skills of elementary students. | Present the problem stimulus via multimedia: use real photos at school, short 30-second video clips, or real objects in class. |
| **Phase 5 (Reflection) is often cut short due to lack of time** | Time management gets drained by turn-based group presentations in Phase 4. | Limit presentation time using Gallery Walk or Marketplace Activity methods so all groups are active simultaneously, then save at least 15 minutes specifically for metacognitive evaluation. |

::tip
The key to a successful Phase 5 at the elementary level is simplifying metacognitive reflection language. Instead of using heavy theoretical terms, ask students these two concrete questions: "Which initial guess turned out wrong after you checked directly in the field?" and "If you solve a similar problem next week, which work steps will your group improve?"
::

::faq
  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: What is the main difference between PBL and Project Based Learning (PjBL)?
  ---
  PBL focuses on analyzing the resolution of real problems (problem resolution), where the final product only serves as a tool to communicate solutions. In contrast, PjBL focuses on designing and producing real works (end-product) which generally take longer to complete.
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: Can lower grade elementary students (Phase A) use PBL?
  ---
  Yes, but the problems raised must be very concrete and right in front of the students' eyes (for example, organizing toys or books in a cramped class locker). Also, learning must use direct physical manipulatives with intensive teacher guidance (guided inquiry).
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: How do you build a formative assessment rubric while the PBL syntax is running?
  ---
  Use a process observation sheet in Phase 3 to monitor three main indicators directly: student activeness during investigation, teamwork collaboration skills, and observation data-based reasoning accuracy.
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: Why does PBL fit the Kemendikdasmen's Deep Learning concept?
  ---
  Because PBL stages fulfill the three Deep Learning pillars: Mindful (students are aware of their thinking steps when evaluating solutions in Phase 5), Meaningful (the problem raised directly relates to real life around the students in Phase 1), and Joyful (students feel joy when exploring and successfully solving challenges in Phases 3 and 4).
  :::
::

::conclusion
The success of **applying Problem Based Learning and PBL syntax** in the classroom is not measured by how fancy the crafts students pin on the wall are, but by the sharpness of their reasoning when connecting field evidence with lesson concepts.

As an action step to prepare your lessons for next week:
- **Pick one upcoming topic:** Take one Learning Objective from Science and Social Studies, Mathematics, or Indonesian Language that demands contextual reasoning according to the latest Learning Outcomes.
- **Find a real problem in the school environment:** Observe the class corner, school garden, or gate area, then turn those findings into an open-ended trigger scenario (ill-structured problem) like in the three case studies above.
- **Secure reflection time in Phase 5:** Use the Marketplace Activity method in Phase 4 so you always have at least the last 15 minutes to clear up misconceptions and guide Mindful Learning reflection with students.
::
