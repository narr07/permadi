**Differentiated instruction is** a pedagogical approach rooted in the awareness that every student has different levels of learning readiness, interests, and learning profiles. Teachers proactively modify the content, activity flow, and learning products so all students get fair access to master essential concepts in the classroom.

This concept, formulated by education expert Carol Ann Tomlinson (2001, 2017), rejects the one-size-fits-all instruction method. In real classrooms, we face a wide variety of student abilities in a single study group every day. This approach exists not to add to the teacher's administrative pile, but to provide a logical framework so students who do not yet understand are not left behind, and advanced students do not feel bored.

## Is Differentiated Instruction a Model or an Approach?

Confusion over terminology often arises when teachers design instructional materials or discuss instructional design. Many educators ask whether differentiated instruction is a learning model or an approach. The answer is firm: **differentiated instruction is an approach** or a philosophical framework, not a learning model.

![Visual diagram comparing a flexible pedagogical lens approach with a rigid linear stepped model flow.](/article/edu-pembelajaran-berdiferensiasi-ilustrasi.webp)

If you want to examine these conceptual boundaries entirely, please read the review on the [differences between learning approaches, models, strategies, methods, and techniques](/blog/differences-approach-model-strategy-method-technique-learning). Briefly, learning models like Problem-Based Learning (PBL), Project-Based Learning (PjBL), or Discovery Learning have standard operational syntax or steps from the beginning to the end of a lesson.

On the other hand, the differentiation approach is a pedagogical principle or lens that you attach to any learning model. You can apply the PBL model while inserting differentiation during the problem presentation stage or group investigation.

| Comparison Aspect | Differentiated Instruction Approach | Learning Model (PBL, PjBL, Inquiry) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Conceptual Nature** | A perspective or philosophical framework in responding to student diversity. | A procedural framework that regulates the course of learning activities from opening to closing. |
| **Operational Syntax** | Has no rigid syntax; flexible to suit classroom needs. | Has standard stages (for example, 5 PBL syntax stages or 6 PjBL stages). |
| **Main Focus** | Adjusting content, process, product, and learning environment based on student profiles. | Achieving competencies through problem-solving, project, or discovery pathways. |
| **How to Use** | Integrated into various teaching models and methods. | Chosen as the main framework for learning scenarios in Teaching Modules. |

## The Foundation of Differentiation in the Merdeka Curriculum and Deep Learning

The principle of differentiation has a clear regulatory umbrella in the Indonesian education system. The Education Standards, Curriculum, and Assessment Agency (BSKAP) of the Ministry of Education officially integrates this principle into the guidelines for learning and assessment across early childhood, basic, and secondary education.

In the Merdeka Curriculum, differentiation intersects directly with the concept of Teaching at the Right Level (TaRL). As explained in the official teacher guides published by the Ministry of Education, teachers no longer have to rigidly complete all chapters in a textbook. Teachers use initial assessment data to align instruction with the students' real competency levels in the classroom.

As the national curriculum strengthens its direction toward Deep Learning pedagogy (learning that is mindful, meaningful, and joyful), differentiation becomes even more essential. Deep Learning requires students to understand concepts profoundly, not just memorize uniform surface facts. When teachers adjust materials to student readiness, critical thinking can fully occur because the given challenges fall exactly within the reach of their abilities.

::note
Official BSKAP teacher guide documents confirm that **teachers do not have to apply all three elements of differentiation (content, process, product) simultaneously in one meeting**. You are free to choose just one aspect that is most relevant to the day's Learning Objective to keep the class manageable.
::

## Three Pillars of Mapping Student Learning Needs

Before modifying instruction, Carol Ann Tomlinson emphasizes the importance of mapping three main characteristics of learners. Without this mapping, teacher modifications will only be random guesses.

### 1. Learning Readiness
Learning readiness is not a measure of innate intelligence (IQ), but the level of mastery of prerequisite knowledge or skills for the specific material taught today. A student might be at an advanced readiness level when learning fraction arithmetic, but at a beginner level when writing a narrative text.

### 2. Student Interest
Interest relates to things that trigger student curiosity and enthusiasm. Connecting lesson concepts to personal hobbies or interests (like sports, drawing, music, or digital games) will increase their intrinsic motivation when working on assignments.

### 3. Learning Profile
A learning profile refers to the most effective way for a student to absorb and process information. This factor depends on learning modality preferences (visual, auditory, or [kinesthetic learning methods](/blog/kinesthetic-learning-methods)), cultural influences, and classroom atmosphere preferences (working independently in a quiet corner or discussing in groups).

## Four Elements of Differentiated Instruction and Practical Examples

To understand the concept of differentiated instruction and its application in the classroom, we need to examine four curricular elements that teachers can modify: content, process, product, and learning environment.

### 1. Content Differentiation
Content relates to what material we teach or how students access that information. The Learning Objective remains the same for the whole class, but the teacher adjusts the complexity of the learning materials or the mode of delivery.

Referring to practical examples in primary school teacher guides, here is the concrete application of content differentiation in class:
* **Beginner Group:** Students who still need reinforcement of basic concepts read picture modules or simple infographics equipped with a glossary of important terms. The teacher can also open a 10-minute mini-lesson specifically for this group.
* **Intermediate Group:** Students read standard explanatory texts from the textbook and answer structured guiding questions.
* **Advanced Group:** Students who have mastered the prerequisite materials analyze real case study articles (tiered texts) that require abstract thinking and complex problem-solving.

### 2. Process Differentiation
The process refers to how students make sense of, process, and practice mastering the concepts they are studying. At this stage, the variation lies in the learning activities and the level of scaffolding provided by the teacher.

Adapting strategies from teaching guidelines, teachers can implement process differentiation through:
* **Learning Centers:** The teacher prepares several activity corners in the classroom. Students rotate or choose the station that fits their needs.
* **Variations in Tools and Media:** When learning about geometric shapes or science experiments, some students use physical manipulative props (wooden blocks or paper nets). Meanwhile, students who already think abstractly directly use digital simulations or analytical worksheets.
* **Gradation of Teacher Guidance:** The beginner group receives step-by-step direct assistance from the teacher, while the advanced group works independently using task card guides.

### 3. Product Differentiation
The product is tangible evidence that students show to demonstrate their understanding after going through a series of learning processes. As emphasized in the curriculum guidelines, the learning objective achievement criteria (assessment rubric) must remain equal, but the task output format follows the student's interest or learning profile.

Example of product differentiation application for an Energy Saving Campaign topic in primary or middle school:
* **Students with visual-design tendencies:** Create infographic posters, comic strips, or manual and digital flowcharts.
* **Students with auditory-verbal tendencies:** Produce voice recordings (short podcasts), write speech texts, or present oral reviews in front of the class.
* **Students with kinesthetic-mechanical tendencies:** Perform a roleplay simulation or assemble a simple three-dimensional model from recycled items.

### 4. Learning Environment
The learning environment includes the psychological climate and the physical layout of the classroom. A classroom that supports differentiation provides flexible seating arrangements: group tables for collaboration and quiet areas for students who need full concentration during independent reading. Psychologically, teachers build a classroom culture that values individual growth so no student feels inferior for working on a different worksheet than their peers.

| Differentiation Element | Teacher Modification Focus | Practical Example in Class |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Content** | Information access and learning material complexity. | Providing tiered texts, explanation videos, and illustrated vocabulary cards. |
| **Process** | How students process concepts and scaffolding intensity. | Using learning centers, varying the duration of teacher assistance, and choosing physical props vs. simulations. |
| **Product** | Final task format to prove competency mastery. | Letting students choose a final report in the form of an essay, infographic poster, video recording, or 3D model with the same concept rubric. |
| **Environment** | Physical room layout and psychological classroom atmosphere. | Arranging chairs into discussion zones and quiet zones, and applying rules of mutual respect for different learning paces. |

## Advanced Strategies: Maintaining Academic Quality

Many differentiation practices in the field get stuck on activities that look lively on the surface but fail to lead students to a complete understanding of concepts. As practitioners, we need to integrate two instructional design principles so differentiation remains academically sharp.

### Aligning Differentiation with Understanding by Design (UbD)
Grant Wiggins and Jay McTighe in their book *Understanding by Design* remind us that effective learning design must start from the end goal (backward design). Before thinking about diverse activity or product variations, teachers must set the Big Ideas and valid assessment evidence first.

Differentiation is a variation of routes to the same mountain peak, not a compromise on essential competency standards. When you arrange a [Merdeka Teaching Module](/blog/merdeka-teaching-module-guide), make sure all content and process variations still lead to equal Learning Outcome indicators.

### Preventing the Matthew Effect through Dynamic Grouping
Without careful group management, a differentiated classroom is prone to the Matthew Effect. This sociological phenomenon describes a condition where advanced students excel even further because they receive high-quality challenges, while students with low readiness fall further behind because they continually only get low-level tasks (the rich get richer, the poor get poorer).

To prevent this gap, curriculum guides suggest flexible and dynamic grouping. Never lock students in fixed groups throughout the semester. Combine homogeneous groups (when the teacher provides specific guidance on basic reading or math) with heterogeneous groups (during collaborative projects). In heterogeneous groups, give central roles to students who still need academic assistance, for example, as logistics coordinators or group spokespersons, to keep their self-efficacy and engagement intact.

## Practical Steps to Implement Differentiated Instruction in Class

For those of you who want to start or are organizing classroom management, follow this four-step workflow so applying differentiation does not drain your teaching preparation time.

::steps
### Conduct Non-Invasive Initial Assessments
You do not need to create pages of written exams to map student readiness. Use simple [formative assessments](/blog/formative-assessment-in-classroom) before starting a new chapter, such as a 3-question trigger quiz, a short raise-hand survey, or observing student responses to an open-ended question on the board. From this brief data, map students into three readiness categories: beginner, intermediate, and advanced.

### Choose One Focus Differentiation Element
Avoid changing content, process, and product all at once in a single meeting. As advised in teaching guides, start with **process differentiation** first. Prepare the same material, then vary the way you provide support in class. Once comfortable, experiment with product or content differentiation in the following meetings.

### Form Flexible Grouping
Arrange student positions according to the goal of the day's activity. Use readiness-based groups when you want to provide intensive scaffolding to the beginner group. Rearrange the group composition into a mix of interests or heterogeneous abilities during problem-solving discussion sessions to spark peer tutoring.

### Evaluate and Adjust Groups Regularly
Use observations during class hours (assessment for learning) and exit tickets at the end of class to monitor student progress. If a student in the beginner group shows strong concept mastery, immediately move them to a group with higher challenges in the next meeting.
::

## Four Common Misconceptions and How to Avoid Them

Many teachers feel overwhelmed by the term differentiated instruction due to circulating misunderstandings. Here are the four most common misconceptions and how to overcome them:

1. **Misconception: Teachers must create 30 different Teaching Modules for 30 students.**
   Education researchers confirm that differentiation is not one-on-one individualized learning. You still compile one Teaching Module with one Learning Objective, but within its activity scenario, you provide 2 to 3 different pathways to accommodate student readiness groups.
2. **Misconception: Students who finish fast get more questions.**
   Giving dozens of additional similar questions to students who are already advanced is not differentiation, it is a punishment for smart kids. The solution is qualitative differentiation (depth and complexity): give them open-ended problem-solving questions with a higher cognitive level, or assign them as peer tutors.
3. **Misconception: Permanently labeling students ("smart group" vs. "slow group").**
   Rigid grouping will damage a child's learning mentality. Remember that learning readiness is contextual to the topic. A student who is slow in abstract numerical calculations might be very adept when designing visual or kinesthetic-based projects. Groups must always be fluid and change according to the material.
4. **Misconception: You must differentiate content, process, and product simultaneously.**
   Forcing all three elements to run concurrently in a single class hour will make classroom management spin out of control. Apply it gradually by choosing the single most urgent element for that meeting.

::warning
Avoid using popular learning style tests (like strictly labeling a child as a "visual learner" so they are never invited to oral discussions) as the sole basis for differentiation. Prioritize mapping based on **learning readiness** for prerequisite materials, because this factor has the most direct impact on student academic success in class.
::

  ---
  No. Simply create one main assessment rubric that measures the essential concept understanding indicators from the Learning Objective.
