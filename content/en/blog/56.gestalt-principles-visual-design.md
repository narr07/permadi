**Gestalt principles in design** are a set of visual perception laws in psychology that explain how the human brain organizes, simplifies, and groups separate visual elements into a complete whole. Instead of processing every line, dot, or text in isolation, the human eye instantly looks for orderly patterns to understand information quickly.

For graphic designers, web developers, and educators creating learning materials, understanding how this perception works forms the foundation of visual communication. When a layout feels clean, easy to scan, and intuitive without long-winded instructions, the design almost certainly applies Gestalt principles correctly.

## Why Does the Human Brain Seek Complete Patterns?

The word *Gestalt* comes from German, meaning shape, pattern, or unified configuration. Psychologist Max Wertheimer first formulated this visual grouping theory scientifically in his 1923 publication (*Untersuchungen zur Lehre von der Gestalt, II*), which Kurt Koffka later expanded in his 1935 book, *Principles of Gestalt Psychology*. Later, between 1992 and 1999, Stephen Palmer extended the theory by adding the concepts of common region and uniform connectedness.

Jon Yablonski explains in his book *Laws of UX* that humans have an innate tendency to see the overall shape first before noticing the details that make it up. One famous phrase summarizes this principle: "The whole is other than the sum of its parts."

When we look at a web page, the brain does not read pixel by pixel. It instantly groups navigation buttons at the top, unifies product cards in the middle, and separates the footer at the bottom. If you master [core graphic design principles for effective layouts](/blog/principles-of-graphic-design-guide), these Gestalt laws act as a bridge between visual aesthetics and human cognitive psychology.

In practice, designers apply Gestalt principles for three main purposes:

1. **Reducing Cognitive Load**: William Lidwell and Kritina Holden emphasize in *Universal Principles of Design* that visual grouping makes it easier for the audience to scan information structures instantly without having to analyze elements one by one.
2. **Building Natural Visual Hierarchy**: Dario Benevento mentions in his book *Frontend Development (UIUX & Psychology)* that spacing and shape similarity direct the user's eye to the main focal point before shifting to secondary information.
3. **Creating Order Without Visual Clutter**: Ellen Lupton and Jennifer C. Phillips show in *Graphic Design: The New Basics* how negative space (whitespace) and shape consistency can group components without relying on dividers or excessive boxes.

## 7 Gestalt Principles in Design You Must Master

To apply them to application interfaces, posters, or brand identities, we need to break down the seven main laws that make up **Gestalt principles in design**.

![Visual comparison diagram of geometric shapes demonstrating seven perceptual grouping principles](/article/art-gestalt-design-principles-ilustrasi.webp)

| Gestalt Principle | Main Visual Trigger | Perception Formed in the Brain | Real Application Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Proximity** | Whitespace distance between elements | Adjacent elements are perceived as one functional group | Tight spacing between a form label and its input field |
| **Similarity** | Color, shape, size, or typography | Similar elements are perceived as having equal roles or levels | All primary call to action (CTA) buttons use a solid blue color |
| **Figure-Ground** | Contrast in color value, size, and negative space | Separation of the main focus object from its background | A modal pop-up window over a darkened screen |
| **Closure** | Empty gaps in familiar shape contours | The brain completes missing parts to form a whole shape | The panda body silhouette in the WWF logo |
| **Continuity** | Flow of lines, curves, or directional alignment | The eye follows a smooth, unbroken path | A horizontal step-by-step checkout indicator |
| **Prägnanz** (Simplicity) | Symmetry and basic shape simplification | Complex objects are simplified into the most stable forms | UI icons based on basic geometric shapes |
| **Common Region** | Border lines, container boxes, or background colors | Elements within one frame are seen as closely related | Card UI components and Bento Grid layouts |

### 1. Proximity
The law of proximity states that the human eye perceives objects close to each other as an interrelated group compared to objects placed further apart. William Lidwell and Kritina Holden rank this principle as the most effective way to structure text information without adding any decorative elements.

In web page design or student worksheets, the distance between a subheading and the paragraph below it must be tighter than the distance from the subheading to the previous paragraph. This spacing instantly tells the reader that the subheading covers the text right underneath.

### 2. Similarity
Users assume visual elements sharing similar attributes like color, shape, size, or font style have equal functions and hierarchy. As Dario Benevento explains, visual consistency makes users immediately understand how to interact with the system.

When readers see blue underlined text in an article, they know it is a link. If all secondary action buttons use an outline style, users do not have to guess their function when moving between pages.

::warning
**Pay Attention to Color Accessibility (WCAG 1.4.1):** When applying the Similarity principle, do not rely solely on color to distinguish element functions. WCAG accessibility standards require additional identifiers like font weight, icon shape, or borders so color-blind users can still recognize the grouped elements.
::

### 3. Figure-Ground
The figure-ground principle explains the human eye's ability to separate the main focal object (figure) from its surrounding background area (ground). Ellen Lupton and Jennifer C. Phillips emphasize that negative space is not wasted empty area, but rather an active element that defines the boundaries of the main object.

In modern interfaces, this principle is clearly visible when a modal dialog box appears on the screen. Designers intentionally add a transparent dark layer (backdrop overlay) to the page background so the user's eyes immediately lock onto the dialog box in the front.

### 4. Closure
Amolendu H outlines in his book *The Golden Ratio In UX Design* that the human brain dislikes disconnected visual information. When we see a shape that has a gap or a missing part, our perception automatically closes that gap to recognize the complete figure.

This principle of closure saves a lot of screen space. In mobile interface design, designers often leave the last card in a carousel partially cut off at the right edge of the screen. That visual crop triggers the Closure principle in the user's brain and provides a natural hint that more hidden content can be swiped to the left.

### 5. Continuity
The human eye naturally moves along a path of lines, rows of dots, or smooth curves rather than taking sudden turns. We perceive elements arranged in a straight line or smooth curve as an interconnected sequence.

When designing [UI UX design workflows and foundations](/blog/foundations-ui-ux-design-workflow), continuity becomes the backbone of navigation. The alignment of the left margin on article text, rows of top navigation menus, and checkout flow graphics use Continuity to help the reader's eyes glide smoothly from the beginning to the end of the page.

### 6. Prägnanz (Simplicity)
The law of Prägnanz (often called the law of good figure or simplicity) states that humans tend to interpret complex and ambiguous images into the simplest, most symmetrical, and stable forms. Visual perception research summarized by Lidwell and Holden proves that symmetrical compositions require much lower working memory resources, making them faster to process and easier to remember.

This principle is also closely related to cognitive accessibility. For readers or students with dyslexia or ADHD, layouts applying the Prägnanz law prevent sensory overload because the page structure is free of visual distortion.

### 7. Common Region and Uniform Connectedness
Developed by Stephen Palmer, Common Region explains that elements placed inside a closed area (like framed boxes or different background color blocks) are immediately considered a single unit. Meanwhile, Uniform Connectedness states that elements connected directly by physical lines will be perceived as tied together.

These two principles form the main foundation of the Bento Grid design trend popularized by Apple and Microsoft. Through modular boxes with soft background colors, designers can present dozens of complex product features on a single screen without overwhelming users.

## Applying Gestalt Principles to Iconic Logos

Beyond page layouts, visual identity designers use Gestalt principles to create simple logos that hold layered meanings. Because logos must remain recognizable even in small sizes, relying on complex detailed lines actually ruins readability.

Here are four real examples of how world-class brands practice [modern graphic logo design principles](/blog/graphic-logo-design-principles) based on Gestalt psychology:

* **WWF Logo (Closure)**: The top of the panda's head and back in the World Wildlife Fund logo actually lack black outlines and blend directly into the white background. However, the black patches on the ears, eyes, and legs are enough for our brain to close the empty gaps and see a complete panda figure.
* **FedEx Logo (Figure-Ground)**: The letters in "FedEx" are designed with specific tightness. The negative space (ground) formed right between the capital "E" and lowercase "x" creates a sharp arrow silhouette pointing to the right. This hidden arrow symbolizes delivery speed and accuracy without needing to add a new image element.
* **IBM Logo (Closure & Continuity)**: Designed by Paul Rand, the IBM logo consists of eight broken horizontal stripes. Thanks to the tight spacing between lines and the straightness of the horizontal flow (Continuity), our eyes do not read them as a stack of separate lines, but close the gaps (Closure) into three solid letters: I, B, and M.
* **NBC Peacock Logo (Similarity & Common Region)**: The peacock tail in the NBC logo is formed from six identical teardrop shapes (Similarity). Although each feather has a different color to represent their broadcasting divisions, the same basic shape and unification of area (Common Region) keep it intact as a single logo unit.

## Translating Gestalt Principles into Front-End and UI Code

For web developers and digital product designers, understanding **Gestalt principles in design** should not stop on the canvas of a graphic application. We must translate this theory mathematically into CSS code architecture and Design Tokens.

Dario Benevento states that modular spacing systems (multiples of `4px` or `8px` in Figma and CSS variables) are the quantitative implementation of the Proximity and Common Region laws.

::steps
### Set Design Token Scales for Proximity
Create CSS variables that separate internal spacing (between closely related elements) and external spacing (between groups of elements with different functions). Avoid using random numbers.

### Wrap Related Components with Common Region
Use a soft contrast background and rounded corners (`border-radius`) on card containers so users instantly catch the boundaries of information groups without needing thick borders.

### Lock Action Consistency with Similarity
Apply a single uniform visual style class for all equivalent interactive elements, such as matching colors, internal space (`padding`), and typography weight on primary buttons.
::

Here is an example of implementing CSS variables that control Proximity, Common Region, and Similarity on a form card component:

```css
:root {
  /* Gestalt Spacing Scale (Proximity Tokens) */
  --space-tight: 0.5rem;    /* 8px: Very close relationship (Label to Input) */
  --space-group: 1.5rem;    /* 24px: Spacing between fields in one group */
  --space-section: 2.5rem;  /* 40px: Separator between different sections */

  /* Shared Area Tokens (Common Region) */
  --surface-card: #f8fafc;
  --border-subtle: #e2e8f0;
}

.form-card {
  /* Common Region: Unifies all form elements */
  background-color: var(--surface-card);
  border: 1px solid var(--border-subtle);
  border-radius: 0.75rem;
  padding: var(--space-section);
}

.field-group {
  display: flex;
  flex-direction: column;
  /* Proximity: Label to input distance made very tight */
  gap: var(--space-tight);
  margin-bottom: var(--space-group);
}

.btn-primary {
  /* Similarity: Consistent visual attributes for primary actions */
  background-color: #0f172a;
  color: #ffffff;
  font-weight: 600;
  padding: 0.75rem 1.5rem;
  border-radius: 0.5rem;
}
```

With the variable structure above, the development team no longer needs to guess spacing sizes manually. When we lock the distance between the label and input field at `8px` (`--space-tight`), while the distance to the next field is `24px` (`--space-group`), the Proximity law automatically prevents users from filling out the wrong form fields.

## Hierarchy of Strength: What Happens When Gestalt Principles Collide?

One vital aspect rarely discussed is what happens when two Gestalt principles clash on a single screen. For instance, there are two icons placed very close together (Proximity), but one of them is placed inside a framed box with another icon that is far away (Common Region). Which principle wins in the human brain?

Empirically in perception psychology, **Common Region and Uniform Connectedness almost always overpower Proximity and Similarity**.

If you connect two distant points with a physical line (Uniform Connectedness), or frame them inside a single card box (Common Region), the human eye will ignore distance and color factors. The brain still considers them as a strongly tied group.

::tip
**Practical Rules for Arranging Complex Dashboards:**
1. Use **Proximity** (empty space) as the first step to group basic information.
2. Use **Similarity** (color and typography) to mark status or interaction levels.
3. Use **Common Region** (card boxes or borders) only when empty space can no longer separate data density.
::

## 4 Common Gestalt Application Mistakes and Solutions

Although the concept looks simple, many web layouts, educational posters, and applications fail to convey clear messages due to the following four visual traps:

1. **Proximity Trap**: Giving the exact same top and bottom margin spacing (for example, both `16px`) to a subheading. As a result, the subheading appears to float in the middle, and readers are confused whether it belongs to the paragraph above or below it. **Solution**: Set the element's spacing to the section it covers to always be smaller than the distance to the previous section.
2. **Border Clutter**: Placing harsh separator lines between every navigation menu, article list, and table column until the display becomes noisy. **Solution**: Remove excessive borders and replace them with measured whitespace (Proximity) or soft contrast background colors (Common Region).
3. **False Similarity**: Designing static category labels (badges) with striking colors, fully rounded corners, and drop shadows that look exactly like clickable buttons (CTAs). Users will click the static element and feel confused. **Solution**: Clearly distinguish visual styles between static information elements and interactive components.
4. **Figure-Ground Ambiguity Ruining Readability**: Placing heading text directly over a busy background photo without a color-dampening layer (scrim or overlay). The letter contours (figure) will merge with the photo details (ground) and become hard to read. **Solution**: Maintain a minimum contrast ratio of 4.5:1 (according to WCAG AA standards) between the text and its background.
