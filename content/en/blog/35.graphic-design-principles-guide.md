**Design principles** are a set of rules, visual laws, and layout guidelines that dictate how graphic elements like lines, shapes, space, colors, and typography are arranged in a composition. If visual elements are the raw materials, these principles act as the grammar that makes the work communicative, hierarchical, and functional.

Many beginners think good visual work comes solely from artistic intuition or natural talent. When designing visual learning materials or building web app interfaces, the approach that actually works is highly mathematical and measurable.

Bad design does not just look ugly. A messy composition forces the audience's brain to work hard to find the core message and drains their mental energy. When you master the right layout rules, you reduce the cognitive load for your readers or app users.

## Understanding Design Principles and Their Effect on Cognition

Fundamentally, design principles bridge the psychology of human perception with how we deliver information. Human eyes do not scan screens or paper randomly. Our brains constantly look for patterns, group similarities, and prioritize the most prominent objects.

In Gestalt psychology, the human brain naturally groups elements that are close together (Law of Proximity) or share similar shapes and colors (Law of Similarity) as a single logical unit of information. Without clear layout rules, elements on a canvas will fight for attention and confuse the eye.

For graphic designers, frontend developers, and educators creating presentation materials, understanding composition rules stops the habit of eyeballing layouts. You know exactly why a button needs to be a certain size, why paragraph spacing needs more breathing room, and how to guide the audience's eyes from the main headline straight to the Call-to-Action button.

## Nine Core Graphic Design Principles You Must Master

When we break down the foundation of modern layout, it comes down to nine main pillars. These nine rules work simultaneously in every visual piece we see daily, from printed posters to mobile app screens.

![Mechanical balance scale demonstrating symmetrical and asymmetrical distribution of geometric shapes](/article/art-prinsip-desain-grafis-ilustrasi.webp)

### 1. Balance

Balance divides the visual weight inside a canvas so the composition feels stable and not lopsided. Physical size in pixels or centimeters is not the only thing that decides visual weight; color saturation, texture complexity, and contrast also matter a lot.

A common question in design exams and layout practice is: **what design principle has different arrangements on the left and right?** The answer is **asymmetrical balance** or informal balance.

Unlike symmetrical balance, which divides the left and right sides identically like a mirror, asymmetrical balance arranges elements of different shapes or sizes on both sides of the canvas while keeping equal optical weight. Besides symmetrical and asymmetrical, there is also radial balance, which radiates elements from a single center point, like flower petals or a compass.

| Comparison Parameter | Symmetrical Balance (Formal) | Asymmetrical Balance (Informal) | Radial Balance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Element Arrangement** | Left and right sides (or top and bottom) are identical like a mirror image. | Left and right sides have different shapes/sizes but equal optical weight. | Elements radiate or rotate around a single center point. |
| **Psychological Impression** | Calm, official, stable, traditional, and structured. | Dynamic, modern, energetic, and flexible. | Strong focus on the center, moving, and centralized. |
| **Main Advantage** | Easy to create and immediately looks neat. | Guides the eye flow more actively and keeps things interesting. | Draws attention directly to the object in the middle. |
| **Application Examples** | Formal invitations, classical architecture, formal profile pages. | Modern website hero sections, event posters, Bento Grid layouts. | Clock designs, mandalas, pie charts, badge logos. |

::tip
**Understanding Optical Weight vs Geometric Size:**
Do not calculate balance just by pixel area. A small bright red or solid black circle on the right side has enough optical weight to balance a large pale gray box on the left side.
::

### 2. Visual Hierarchy

Visual hierarchy ranks elements based on their level of importance. This principle guides the audience's eyes to digest information in steps: what to read first, second, and last.

Without a clear hierarchy, all text and images will look flat. You can build hierarchy through different font sizes (headlines are always larger than body text), font weight, top page placement, and color contrast.

### 3. Contrast

Contrast happens when two opposing elements sit next to each other, like bright colors on a dark background, thick fonts next to thin fonts, or rigid geometric shapes near curving organic forms. Its main function is to maintain legibility and separate the object focus from its background.

In modern digital product development, contrast is no longer just an aesthetic choice, but a mandatory accessibility standard. Based on the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 from the W3C, normal size text must have a minimum contrast ratio of **4.5:1** against its background color (Level AA standard). Meanwhile, large text (minimum 18pt or 14pt bold) and UI graphic components need a minimum contrast ratio of **3:1** to remain readable for visually impaired users. You can learn how to pick the right color combinations through [the definitive guide to color theory in visual arts](/blog/color-theory-in-visual-arts).

### 4. Emphasis or Focal Point

If hierarchy organizes the overall reading order, emphasis creates a single main center of interest (focal point). This is the part that first catches the audience's eye when they look at your work.

The rule of thumb is simple: one canvas can only have one "king". If you highlight the title, illustration, discount badge, and signup button with the exact same size and striking colors, not a single element will manage to lead the attention.

### 5. Proportion and Scale

Scale refers to the size of an object compared to a standard size or the overall canvas space, while proportion is the ratio relationship between parts within a single composition.

Designers often use the mathematical comparison of the **Golden Ratio (1 : 1.618)** to divide layout space or set font scales. When the width of the main content column has a 1.618 ratio to the sidebar width, the human eye sees that proportion as a harmonious and natural arrangement.

### 6. Rhythm and Repetition

Repetition means reusing the same or similar visual elements across the entire work, like consistent icon styles, color palettes, fonts, and spacing. Regular repetition creates visual rhythm.

In frontend coding and Design Systems, this repetition and rhythm principle translates directly into design tokens. We set a spacing scale in multiples of 4 or 8 pixels (`4px`, `8px`, `16px`, `24px`, `32px`) so the distance between cards and components forms a consistent rhythm across the whole app page.

### 7. Whitespace (Negative Space)

Whitespace or negative space is the canvas area left without active text or images. This empty space does not have to be white; it can be any background color surrounding the main objects.

Do not think of whitespace as wasted area. This section works as a visual noise canceler, giving the reader's eyes breathing room, and emphasizing the borders between information groups according to the Gestalt law of proximity.

### 8. Alignment

Alignment arranges elements along an imaginary axis line (left, right, center, or top to bottom) or inside a grid system structure. Precise alignment creates an invisible connection between elements even if their positions are far apart.

Look at the legendary [Swiss Style design](/blog/what-is-swiss-style-design) approach or International Typographic Style. This design style relies entirely on a strict mathematical grid system and flush-left, ragged-right text alignment, resulting in a very neat, objective, and scannable look without needing excessive decoration.

### 9. Unity and Harmony

Unity is the end goal of all the **design principles** above. A piece of work achieves unity when all its elements look tied together, support the same theme, and no component looks like it was forced in. Unity transforms separate text snippets, boxes, and photos into one complete visual entity.

## Applying Design Principles in Modern UI and Visual Trends

The classic layout rules above never get old. These principles keep adapting to new mediums, from the print era to interactive screens. If you observe the [guide to graphic and modern UI design styles](/blog/guide-to-graphic-and-ui-design-styles) popular today, they all stand on the foundation of the same principles.

Take the **Bento Grid** trend that is popular on tech product landing pages as an example. This layout divides information into modular boxes of varying sizes like a Japanese lunch box. Theoretically, the Bento Grid combines asymmetrical balance, modular proportion, and the Common Region law in Gestalt psychology, where elements inside a clearly bordered box are perceived as a single feature group.

Another example is the **Neo-Brutalism** style on the modern web. This style intentionally breaks the smoothness of minimalist design by showing extreme contrast: raw saturated background colors, thick solid black borders, bold typography, and hard offset shadows without blur effects. Even though it looks rebellious, good Neo-Brutalism still obeys visual hierarchy and strict grid alignment so the interface remains comfortable to use.

## Practical Steps to Apply Design Principles from Scratch

Understanding the theory alone is not enough when you face a blank canvas in Figma, Canva, or a CSS code editor. Follow these measurable steps so every visual project you make meets strong composition standards.

::steps
### Set Up a Grid and Asymmetrical Balance
Do not just place elements randomly in the middle of the canvas. Turn on a grid system (like 12 columns for the web or an 8-point grid system for spacing). If you design a hero section, apply asymmetrical balance: place a large product image or illustration on the left side, then balance the right side with a bold headline, a short intro paragraph, a high-contrast action button, and 20% to 30% more whitespace.

### Build Hierarchy with Modular Typographic Scales
Avoid guessing font sizes manually. Use a modular type scale ratio, for example, the Major Third ratio (a multiplier of **1.250**) with a base text size of **16px**. Calculate the font sizes in steps:
- Body text: **16px**
- Heading 3 (H3): 16 × 1.25 = **20px**
- Heading 2 (H2): 20 × 1.25 = **25px**
- Heading 1 (H1): 25 × 1.25 = **31.25px** (round it to **32px**)

### Distribute Colors with the 60-30-10 Rule (HSL Model)
Use the HSL (Hue, Saturation, Lightness) color format so you can easily control brightness and contrast. Divide the canvas color proportion into three parts:
- **60% Dominant Color:** Use this for the main background space. Pick a neutral color with very high Lightness (95% to 98%) for light themes, or very low (10% to 12%) for dark themes.
- **30% Secondary Color:** Apply this to component cards, navigation bars, and heading typography to form visual structure.
- **10% Accent Color (Focal Point):** Use the color with the most striking saturation and contrast specifically for the main Call-to-Action button or important emphasis elements.

### Test Contrast and Consistency Through Code
Tie all those scale, color, and alignment rules into a consistent system, then test the contrast ratio using standard WCAG checker tools. If you apply this to a web project, save these values into CSS variables (design tokens) so the design rhythm and proportions are kept automatically.
::

Here is an example of CSS variable implementation that translates the Major Third scale hierarchy principle, 8-point grid spacing rhythm, and HSL color proportions:

```css
:root {
  /* 1. Proportion & Color Contrast Principle (60-30-10 Rule in HSL) */
  --bg-dominant-60: hsl(210, 20%, 98%);       /* Clean background (Lightness 98%) */
  --surface-secondary-30: hsl(215, 25%, 18%); /* Text & structure (Contrast > 4.5:1) */
  --accent-focal-10: hsl(16, 85%, 52%);       /* Focal point / Main CTA */

  /* 2. Visual Hierarchy Principle (Modular Scale 1.250 - Major Third) */
  --font-body: 1rem;       /* 16px */
  --font-h3: 1.25rem;      /* 20px (16 × 1.25) */
  --font-h2: 1.563rem;     /* 25px (20 × 1.25) */
  --font-h1: 2rem;         /* 32px (rounded from 31.25px) */

  /* 3. Rhythm, Repetition & Whitespace Principle (8-pt Grid System) */
  --space-xs: 0.5rem;      /* 8px */
  --space-sm: 1rem;        /* 16px */
  --space-md: 1.5rem;      /* 24px */
  --space-lg: 2rem;        /* 32px */
  --space-xl: 3rem;        /* 48px */
}

.asymmetrical-hero {
  display: grid;
  grid-template-columns: 1.618fr 1fr; /* Golden Ratio proportion */
  gap: var(--space-xl);
  padding: var(--space-xl);
  background-color: var(--bg-dominant-60);
  color: var(--surface-secondary-30);
  align-items: center;
}
```

## Common Mistakes When Applying Graphic Design Principles

Even designers who are used to graphic software often fall into these fundamental mistakes when putting together a layout:

1. **Horror Vacui Syndrome (Fear of Empty Space):** Many visual creators feel like they are losing out if they see empty areas on the canvas, so they cram in ornaments, stickers, border lines, or enlarge text until it fills the screen edges. As a result, the audience experiences visual fatigue because there are no pauses between information.
2. **False Contrast That Ignores Accessibility:** To chase a soft minimalist aesthetic, designers often pair light gray text on a white background, or bright yellow text on a light background. This combination fails to meet the WCAG minimum 4.5:1 ratio and strains the reader's eyes, especially under bright glare.
3. **Flat Hierarchy:** When a creator enlarges the title, enlarges the price, bolds all feature points, and puts a blazing red color on the contact number all at once, the end result is total noise. If all elements shout for attention, not a single message is heard clearly.
4. **Manual Alignment Without a Grid:** Nudging boxes and text just by estimating with the eye often makes the left edge of a title slightly miss the left edge of the paragraph below it. Ordinary audiences might not notice this tiny untidiness directly, but subconsciously it lowers the credibility of your work.

::warning
**The Trap of Aesthetics Over Function:**
Never sacrifice text readability for visual style. No matter how cool the shadow effects or asymmetrical layouts you create, the design fails functionally if readers struggle to find the main information quickly.

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: What is the design principle with different arrangements on the left and right called?
  ---
  This principle is called **asymmetrical balance** or informal balance. Even though elements on the left and right sides of the canvas have different shapes, sizes, or quantities, they are still arranged in a way that creates a balanced optical weight.
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: What is the difference between design elements and design principles?
  ---
  Design elements are the physical components or visual raw materials seen directly on the canvas, including dots, lines, planes or shapes, textures, colors, typography, and space. Conversely, design principles are the rules, formulas, or ways to arrange those physical elements (like setting contrast, balance, and hierarchy) to form a harmonious and communicative work.
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: Why is asymmetrical balance used more often in modern design than symmetrical?
  ---
  Symmetrical balance does give a neat and formal impression, but it often feels stiff, static, and predictable. Modern design (especially web interfaces, apps, and digital posters) prefers asymmetrical balance because it can create a dynamic and flexible feel, and is more effective at guiding the audience's eye movement from one focal point to the next piece of information.
  :::

  :::faq-item
  ---
  question: What is the minimum color contrast ratio for an easy-to-read design?
  ---
  Based on the international accessibility standard WCAG 2.2 Level AA, normal size text must have a minimum contrast ratio of **4.5:1** against its background color. As for large text (minimum 18pt regular or 14pt bold) and UI graphic elements, the minimum contrast ratio limit is **3:1**.
  :::

::conclusion
Mastering **design principles** is not about memorizing theoretical definitions for an exam, but training your eyes to manage the audience's cognitive load. Evaluate the design draft, presentation material, or web page you are working on today with three quick checks:
1. **Hierarchy Test (Squint Test):** Squint your eyes when looking at the screen. Does one main focal point still stand out, or does everything blend into a flat gray?
2. **Space and Alignment Audit:** Turn on the grid guides and make sure every element aligns on a strict axis, and give enough whitespace around important information.
3. **Contrast Verification:** Use a color contrast checker tool to ensure all text meets the minimum 4.5:1 ratio.

If you want to study how these layout rules work more specifically in interface compositions, continue reading the [core graphic design principles for effective layouts](/blog/principles-of-graphic-design-guide) on this blog.
