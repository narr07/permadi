**Color theory in art** is a set of optical principles, pigment mixing rules, and psychological guidelines that dictate how humans arrange and perceive visual spectrums. The art curriculum summarizes this concept through four main references: Sir Isaac Newton's light spectrum experiments, David Brewster's pigment classification, Albert H. Munsell's color space dimensions, and Louis Prang's color wheel harmony system.

Many art students and beginner designers think picking colors is purely instinctual. When their paintings look dull or their web layouts strain the eyes, they struggle to find what went wrong.

In reality, color follows highly measurable laws of physics and human biology. Understanding this theoretical foundation will change how we mix paint on a palette or set color codes on a digital canvas.

## Exploring 4 Color Theories in Art by Key Figures

When academic discussions ask us to explain color theory in art, the most accurate references point to four scientists and art educators. Each figure built a framework from a different perspective, ranging from light physics to paint pigment mixtures.

### 1. Sir Isaac Newton's Light Spectrum Theory (1666-1704)

Sir Isaac Newton laid the foundation of modern color science through his glass prism experiment in 1666, which he later published in *Opticks* (1704). Newton proved that white sunlight is not pure white but rather a combination of the rainbow spectrum: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, and violet.

Newton then connected the ends of this spectrum into a circle, creating the world's first color wheel. Newton's approach is based on **additive color** (light). If those seven light spectrums are recombined through a lens, they turn back into bright white light.

### 2. David Brewster's Pigment Theory (1831)

While Newton focused on light, Scottish scientist David Brewster translated color concepts into physical materials (paint pigments) in 1831. The Indonesian art reference book by Sulasmi Darma Prawira (1989), *Color: Theory and Its Creative Application*, places Brewster's theory as the most popular foundation in our education curriculum.

Brewster simplified colors in nature into four pigment groups (**subtractive color**): primary, secondary, tertiary, and neutral colors. We apply this theory directly when mixing watercolor, acrylic, or poster paint in the classroom.

### 3. Albert H. Munsell's Three-Dimensional Color Theory (1905)

American painter and art teacher Albert H. Munsell realized that a flat, two-dimensional color wheel was not enough to fully explain color properties. In 1905, Munsell introduced a three-dimensional color space system that became the origin of modern digital color spaces (HSL/HSV).

Munsell divided color anatomy into three measurable dimensions:
* **Hue**: The identity or name of the color spectrum itself, such as red, yellow, or green.
* **Value**: The lightness or darkness of a color based on light reflection, moving from pitch black at the bottom to pure white at the top.
* **Chroma**: The purity, intensity, or saturation of a color, measured from neutral gray at the center axis toward vivid pure color at the outer edge.

### 4. Louis Prang's Color Wheel System (1876)

Louis Prang refined Brewster's classification into the printing and art education system through the *Prang Color Chart* (1876). Prang popularized the 12-segment color wheel chart that maps the precise positions of primary, secondary, and intermediate colors.

Prang's biggest contribution was formulating geometric harmony schemes on the color wheel. Thanks to Prang's system, artists and designers have clear formulas to draw a straight line (complementary), a triangle (triadic), or adjacent lines (analogous) when building a palette.

## Brewster's Classification of 3 Pigment Color Levels

Many students and teachers look up details about the **3 levels of color theory in art** when preparing study materials. In Indonesian art modules, including the Merdeka Curriculum, this phrase refers directly to the three hierarchical levels of pigment mixing according to David Brewster.

Understanding these three levels is an absolute requirement before we pick up a brush. You can study the visual map in more detail in the [Primary Secondary Tertiary Color Wheel Design Guide](/blog/primary-secondary-tertiary-color-wheel).

### 1. Primary Colors (First Basic Colors)

Primary colors are pure colors that stand alone and cannot be created by mixing any other colors. Instead, these three act as the parent colors that produce all other color variations on the canvas. In traditional paint-based art (RYB model), the primary colors are **Red**, **Yellow**, and **Blue**.

### 2. Secondary Colors (Second Derivative Colors)

Secondary colors are born when you mix two primary colors in equal ratios (1:1). This mixture produces three new colors positioned exactly halfway between their parent colors on the color wheel:
* **Orange**: A mix of Red and Yellow pigments.
* **Green**: A mix of Yellow and Blue pigments.
* **Purple (Violet)**: A mix of Blue and Red pigments.

### 3. Tertiary Colors (Third Derivative Colors)

Tertiary colors are formed by mixing one primary color with an adjacent secondary color on the Prang color wheel. The result is six transitional colors that enrich painting gradients:
* **Red-Orange (Vermilion)**: Red mixed with Orange.
* **Yellow-Orange (Amber)**: Yellow mixed with Orange.
* **Yellow-Green (Chartreuse)**: Yellow mixed with Green.
* **Blue-Green (Teal)**: Blue mixed with Green.
* **Blue-Purple (Indigo)**: Blue mixed with Purple.
* **Red-Purple (Magenta)**: Red mixed with Purple.

::note
**What About Neutral Colors?**
Beyond the three levels above, Brewster included a fourth group: neutral colors (black, white, and gray or earth brown). In pigment theory, mixing all three primary colors (Red + Yellow + Blue) in equal ratios will absorb almost the entire light spectrum, resulting in a muddy color close to dark gray or earth brown.
::

### Scientific Correction: Why is RYB Different From CMYK and RGB?

When we explain color theory in art in the classroom, a critical question often arises: why are the primary colors of paint (Red, Yellow, Blue) different from printer ink (CMYK) or phone screens (RGB)?

The answer lies in how an object's surface processes light:
1. **RYB Model (Red, Yellow, Blue)**: A historical 19th-century system retained in traditional drawing materials. Traditional paint pigments are most intuitively processed using a red, yellow, and blue base to train basic aesthetic sensitivity.
2. **CMYK Model (Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, Key/Black)**: Modern optical science's correction to the RYB model. In subtractive pigment physics, Cyan, Magenta, and Yellow have a much wider and more accurate color gamut. If you mix Magenta and Yellow, you get pure Red; meanwhile, no paint mixture can produce pure Magenta.
3. **RGB Model (Red, Green, Blue)**: An additive color system based on direct light emission on monitors, televisions, and projectors. When Red, Green, and Blue lights are emitted together at full intensity, our eyes perceive them as White.

## Drawing Materials: Practicing Pigment Mixing and Harmony

Understanding the theory on paper will not help much without the motor practice of mixing mediums directly. In drawing and painting, the ability to control the brush and palette relies on how we manipulate Munsell's dimensions (Value and Chroma) while arranging harmony schemes.

::steps
### Prepare a Primary Triangle Palette
Place Red, Yellow, and Blue acrylic or poster paints in a triangle pattern at the edge of the palette. Provide clean water in two separate containers: one for rinsing dirty brushes and another for thinning paint so the Chroma purity is not contaminated by other pigment residues.

### Create Secondary and Tertiary Color Bridges
Take a little Yellow and Blue using a palette knife or clean brush, then mix them halfway between the two colors to produce Green. Do the same to produce Orange and Purple. Avoid mixing paint directly on the drawing paper to prevent the paper fibers from getting damaged by repeated friction.

### Control Tint, Shade, and Tone
After getting the 12 basic colors of the Prang wheel, train your Munsell dimension sensitivity through three modification techniques:
* **Tint (Increasing Value)**: Add white paint little by little to a pure color to create lighter shades or pastel colors.
* **Shade (Decreasing Value)**: Add a little of its complementary color or black paint to darken the color when drawing shadow areas.
* **Tone (Decreasing Chroma)**: Mix neutral gray (white + black) into a pure color to mute its brightness, producing a calm and natural color.

### Apply the 60-30-10 Composition Rule
When starting to color a drawing area or design a visual layout, divide color proportions using the 60-30-10 rule. Allocate **60% dominant color** (usually a calm tone or neutral color) as the background. Use **30% secondary color** for main objects or shape structures. Leave **10% accent color** with the most vivid Chroma as the focal point.
::

### Harmonic Color Schemes and Color Temperature Psychology

Aside from proportion, the direction of lines on the color wheel determines the emotional atmosphere of your work. First, we recognize the division of color temperature. **Warm colors** (red, orange, yellow) have longer optical wavelengths, providing an advancing effect, feeling close, energetic, and attention-grabbing.

On the other hand, **cool colors** (blue, green, purple) have shorter wavelengths that provide a receding effect. Landscape artists use the nature of cool colors to paint distant mountains or backgrounds to create an illusion of spatial depth.

To maintain harmony between elements, use these four color scheme formulas:
* **Monochromatic Scheme**: Plays with a single Hue varied through Tint, Shade, and Tone. This approach is highly popular in [Swiss Style design](/blog/what-is-swiss-style-design) which prioritizes grid order and typography clarity.
* **Analogous Scheme**: Combines 2 to 3 adjacent colors on the color wheel (e.g., yellow, yellow-green, and green). This scheme produces a cohesive visual that is comfortable to look at.
* **Complementary Scheme**: Pairs two colors 180 degrees opposite each other (like blue with orange, or red with green). This high contrast is very effective for highlighting main objects in a painting or Call-to-Action buttons on an app screen.
* **Triadic Scheme**: Draws an equilateral triangle (120-degree angles) on the color wheel. The result is a dynamic and bold combination of three contrasting colors, often used in poster illustrations and [modern graphic design styles](/blog/guide-to-graphic-and-ui-design-styles).

## From Canvas to Digital Screens: Munsell's Bridge to UI/UX and WCAG

Many art discussions stop at brushes and canvases, as if classic color theory is disconnected from information technology. Yet, there is a direct continuity between traditional painting principles and modern interface engineering.

The Value concept formulated by Albert Munsell in 1905 is the main foundation of modern web accessibility standards, namely **WCAG 2.2 (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines)**. When a painter ensures their main object has a contrasting Value against the background, a frontend developer does the exact same thing using luminance ratio calculations.

In the WCAG 2.2 standard, normal-sized text must have a minimum luminance contrast ratio of **4.5:1** against its background color, while graphical interface elements require a minimum ratio of **3:1**. Without a good understanding of Value, application interfaces will be difficult for users to read.

The development of the **CSS Color Module Level 4** specification from W3C now introduces the **Oklab** and **LCH** color spaces. Traditional color spaces like HSL (Hue, Saturation, Lightness) have perceptual flaws: pure yellow (`hsl(60, 100%, 50%)`) looks much brighter to human eyes than pure blue (`hsl(240, 100%, 50%)`), even though both have a Lightness value of 50%.

Oklab and LCH color spaces fix these perceptual flaws by adopting Munsell's color space uniformity principle. When we create a gradient transition or shift Hue without changing Lightness and Chroma values, the visual brightness remains consistent and no longer produces muddy gray dead zones.

```css
/* Comparison of classic CSS color declarations (HSL) vs Modern Perceptual (LCH/Oklab) */
:root {
  /* HSL: Blue and Yellow have 50% Lightness, but their actual brightness is unbalanced */
  --brand-blue-hsl: hsl(240 100% 50%);
  --brand-yellow-hsl: hsl(60 100% 50%);

  /* LCH: 65% Lightness and 70 Chroma keep perceptual brightness equal */
  --surface-primary: lch(65% 70 250); /* Blue with controlled luminance */
  --surface-accent: lch(65% 70 90);   /* Yellow with equal brightness level */
  --text-on-surface: lch(15% 10 250); /* Dark shade to meet WCAG contrast > 4.5:1 */
}
```

## Visual Traps: 3 Common Mistakes When Applying Color Theory

Whether painting on drawing paper or structuring an interface design, there are three classic mistakes that often ruin visual work quality.

### 1. Creating Shadows Only with Pitch Black Paint (Muddy Shadow)

Beginners often take a shortcut by mixing black paint directly into the object's color when trying to draw shadows. For example, mixing yellow with black to create a shadow on a yellow fruit. The result is not a natural shadow, but a muddy color that looks dirty and loses vitality (desaturated mud).

In the real world, shadows are influenced by environmental light reflection and color temperature. To darken a color vibrantly, mix a little of its **complementary color** (add a bit of dark purple into yellow) or shift the Hue toward the nearest cool color (like adding burnt umber brown).

### 2. Ignoring Value Contrast After Being Fooled by Hue

Bright red text on a bright green background often strains the eyes and causes dizziness. This phenomenon is called **chromatic vibration**. Even though red and green are opposite in Hue (complementary), both might have the exact same level of lightness (Value).

::tip
**Grayscale Test**
To check if your painting composition or UI design has a strong shape hierarchy, change your work's photo into black and white. If the boundaries between elements disappear and the main object blends with the background, your Value contrast has issues and needs fixing.
::

### 3. Ignoring the Simultaneous Contrast Effect

French chemist Michel Eugène Chevreul formulated an important optical law: **a color never stands alone**. Human eyes always compare a color with other surrounding colors.

If you place a neutral gray area on a bright red background, the gray will appear to have a greenish bias to the observer's eye. Understanding this optical interaction prevents artists and designers from getting frustrated when paint colors or visual elements seem to change when placed next to other colors.

  ---
  All three are manual techniques to adjust color dimensions. Tint is adding white pigment to increase lightness (Value). Shade is adding a little complementary or black pigment to decrease Value (making shadows). Tone is adding neutral gray to decrease saturation (Chroma) so the color looks calmer.

Mastering **color theory in art** requires a balance between understanding optical science and visual practice experience. Hold onto the basic principles: use Brewster's classification (Primary, Secondary, Tertiary) when measuring physical pigments, apply the Prang wheel to maintain palette harmony, and always check Munsell's Value dimensions so your work has clear contrast.

As a practical step today, prepare three primary paint colors (Red, Yellow, Blue) along with white, then manually create a 12-segment color wheel on drawing paper.
