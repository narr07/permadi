The instruction to apply **critical thinking given an equation** in math pedagogy refers to higher-order thinking skills. It requires students to analyze algebraic relational structures, test variable domain limits, and explore the entire solution space. This approach moves away from mechanical calculation toward structured logical proof.

This prompt is highly familiar to middle school teachers and students using official textbooks from the Indonesian Ministry of Education, both in the 2013 Curriculum and the Merdeka Curriculum Phase D. Unfortunately, many students stop thinking right after they find one matching pair of numbers. They treat this exploration task like a standard multiple-choice question that only asks for a single final answer.

As educators, we need to unpack why this simple instruction successfully separates students who merely memorize formulas from those who truly understand math concepts. We will look at the problem structure, the complete solution steps, and strategies to design teaching modules that spark critical reasoning in class.

## Understanding Critical Thinking Given an Equation in Algebra

Why do modern math textbooks always include a special rubric titled **critical thinking given an equation** in chapters on exponents and linear functions? The answer lies in a new way of viewing the equals sign (=).

![Conceptual flow diagram showing a balanced scale and four analytical stage icons.](/article/edu-aljabar-berpikir-kritis-ilustrasi.webp)

The majority of elementary and middle school students hold an operational view of the equals sign. When they see the = sign, their brains immediately process it as a command button to calculate the final result, much like on a calculator. Conversely, modern algebra education requires a relational view. The equals sign acts as a static balance scale that demands specific logical conditions on both sides.

Based on the critical thinking frameworks of Peter Facione (2015) and Robert Ennis (2011), solving equation-based problems requires four sequential cognitive stages:
1. **Interpretation:** Students read math symbols, identify the applicable algebraic operation properties, and note the domain constraints of the given variables.
2. **Analysis:** Students transform complex equations into simpler, easier-to-test linear relations.
3. **Evaluation:** Students test each candidate variable value against the domain limits to filter valid values and eliminate those that break the rules.
4. **Inference and Explanation:** Students draw conclusions about the total number of possible solutions, then write the logical arguments behind their conclusion.

Research on middle school students' mathematical critical reasoning published by Wulandari and Warmi (2022) in the *Jurnal Teorema* reveals a major field finding. Students generally pass the initial interpretation stage, which involves writing down known information from the problem. However, their performance drops significantly on the evaluation and inference indicators because they fail to prove why a solution set is limited.

### The Shift from Mechanical Calculation to Deep Learning

The Ministry of Primary and Secondary Education under Minister Abdul Mu'ti continues to promote a Deep Learning approach based on three pillars: Mindful Learning, Meaningful Learning, and Joyful Learning.

In a math class context, Deep Learning shifts the focus from surface learning to a complete conceptual understanding. Students no longer just memorize tricks for moving numbers across the equals sign. When working on **critical thinking given an equation** tasks, students practice being mindful of why a variable has an upper limit, understanding meaningful cause-and-effect relationships between numbers, and feeling joyful intellectual satisfaction when they successfully find all answer combinations like a detective solving a puzzle.

This type of question also serves as a bridge to Open-Middle Problems. These problems have a clear starting point and a definite number of final answers, but they give students the freedom in the middle of the process to choose independent exploration strategies. This exact ability is tested in the Computer-Based National Assessment (ANBK) and PISA studies, where students often struggle when facing algebraic models with multiple variables.

| Comparison Aspect | Routine Problems (Procedural) | Critical Thinking Problems (Relational) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cognitive Focus** | Memorizing formulas and calculating quickly (LOTS). | Analyzing relations, testing limits, and proving (HOTS). |
| **Variable Characteristics** | One unknown variable with a single value (x = 5). | Two or more mutually dependent variables within specific limits. |
| **Perception of Sign (=)** | A command to write the calculation result. | A marker of logical equality between the left and right sides. |
| **Final Answer Format** | A single exact number with no sentence explanation needed. | A set of ordered pairs accompanied by a written argument or reason. |
| **Deep Learning Pillar** | Tends to be mechanical (Surface Learning). | Demands full awareness of concept boundaries (Mindful & Meaningful). |

## Case Study and Exponent Problem Solving for Phase D

One of the most popular prototype questions in 9th-grade textbooks or Phase D of the Merdeka Curriculum comes from the exponents chapter.

> **Question:** *Critical Thinking: Given the equation (5ᵐ)/(5ⁿ) = 5⁴. Determine two numbers m and n with values between 1 and 9 that satisfy the equation, and find the number of possible solutions! Explain your reasoning.*

Many students immediately answer m = 6 and n = 2, then consider their task finished. That answer is arithmetically correct, but it only meets a small part of the question's demands. To get a perfect score on an authentic assessment, students must follow systematic problem-solving steps.

::steps
### Algebraic Analysis and Reduction
The first step is to simplify the exponent fraction using the division property of exponents with the same base, which is (aᵐ)/(aⁿ) = aᵐ⁻ⁿ.

By applying this property to the equation, we get:
(5ᵐ)/(5ⁿ) = 5⁴ implies 5ᵐ⁻ⁿ = 5⁴

Since the base numbers on the left and right sides are both 5, we can directly equate the exponents:
m - n = 4

To make it easier to test, we change the equation into an explicit form for m:
m = n + 4

This simple linear equation is the key to unlocking the entire solution space.

### Testing Against Domain Constraints
The second step is to re-read the domain constraints set by the problem. The values of m and n must be integers from 1 to 9. Mathematically, this condition is written as 1 ≤ m ≤ 9 and 1 ≤ n ≤ 9.

Now we substitute the value of n sequentially starting from the lowest limit:
* If n = 1, then m = 1 + 4 = 5 (Meets the condition, since 5 ≤ 9).
* If n = 2, then m = 2 + 4 = 6 (Meets the condition, since 6 ≤ 9).
* If n = 3, then m = 3 + 4 = 7 (Meets the condition, since 7 ≤ 9).
* If n = 4, then m = 4 + 4 = 8 (Meets the condition, since 8 ≤ 9).
* If n = 5, then m = 5 + 4 = 9 (Meets the condition, since 9 ≤ 9).
* If n = 6, then m = 6 + 4 = 10 (Fails the condition, since m exceeds the maximum limit of 9).

The testing stops at n = 5 because any subsequent addition to the value of n will definitely produce an m value above 9.

### Inference and Logical Argument Writing
The final step is to summarize the findings and write the mathematical justification. The ordered pairs (m, n) that satisfy the equation are (5, 1), (6, 2), (7, 3), (8, 4), and (9, 5).

There are exactly **5 unique solution variations**. The logical reason: the difference between m and n must always be 4, while the maximum allowed value for both variables is 9. As a result, the input value for n can only be filled by integers from 1 to 5.
::

::note
Pay attention to the phrase "between 1 and 9 inclusive" in the Indonesian textbook problem. In everyday school Indonesian usage, "sampai dengan" is inclusive (counting 1 and 9). However, in pure mathematical grammar, "between 1 and 9" is often interpreted as exclusive (1 < m, n < 9). Teachers should agree on this limit definition with students early in the lesson to avoid confusion during grading. If interpreted exclusively (1 and 9 are excluded), the pairs (5,1) and (9,5) are dropped, leaving only 3 solutions.
::

## Classroom Learning Scenarios to Spark Critical Reasoning

Handing over the answer key directly on the whiteboard will only make students copy without thinking. For the ability to apply **critical thinking given an equation** to truly grow, teachers need to design a learning flow that gives students room to try, make mistakes, and find their own patterns.

Research by A'izah and Dewi (2024) in the *Jurnal Circle* proves that applying innovative learning models like Problem-Based Learning (PBL) and Project-Based Learning (PjBL) to equation topics in the Merdeka Curriculum yields an N-Gain score of 0.765. This number falls into the high category and is far more effective at improving students' mathematical critical reasoning compared to expository lecture methods.

You can combine [Problem Based Learning stages and practical syntax](/blog/problem-based-learning-stages-and-syntax) or [Discovery Learning and Inquiry stages for class](/blog/discovery-learning-and-inquiry-stages-for-class) with the Deep Learning framework through these three practical steps.

### 1. Mindful Framing (Reflective Apperception)
Do not start the lesson by writing exponent properties on the board. Begin by displaying the equation (5ᵐ)/(5ⁿ) = 5⁴ directly without telling them which formula to use.

Throw out a prompt question that awakens student awareness: *"Do you think the letters m and n in this equation only have one correct value, or do they have many possible answers? How do we prove that no other answers are missed?"*

This kind of question instantly breaks the single-answer bias in students' heads. They realize from the very first minute that this task demands a thorough investigation, not just number guessing.

### 2. Collaborative Inquiry (Differentiated Process)
Once students understand the problem, divide them into discussion groups based on learning readiness mapping (initial diagnostic assessment). Differentiating the process helps ensure that students who struggle with basic counting do not get frustrated, while proficient students do not feel bored.

* **Pioneer Group (Basic Readiness):** Provide concrete visual aids in the form of two sets of number cards numbered 1 to 9. Ask them to physically pair the m card and the n card on the table, then manually map out the multiplication of the number 5 ((5 × 5 × 5 × 5 × 5)/5 = 5⁴) until they discover the exponent subtraction pattern themselves.
* **Capable Group (Intermediate Readiness):** Guide students to manually create a two-variable relation table. They write the list of n values in the left column, then calculate the m values in the right column using the division property of exponents they have already learned.
* **Advanced Group (Enrichment):** Challenge them to formulate limit functions and design new problem variations. For example, what happens to the number of solutions if the domains of m and n are changed to a set of negative integers? What if the equation is changed into a multiplication format like 5ᵐ × 5ⁿ = 5¹⁰?

For fellow teachers who want to speed up concept comprehension in both basic and intermediate groups, this kind of visual grouping technique aligns with [ways to apply Accelerated Learning in Merdeka Curriculum](/blog/accelerated-learning-merdeka-curriculum).

### 3. Argument-Based Authentic Assessment
During the presentation and conclusion session, apply a formative assessment that scores the quality of the argument. Do not just place a checkmark on the number 5 as the final total of solutions.

Require each group to write a **"Mathematical Justification"** section in at least two coherent sentences on their worksheet. Students must be able to explain their reason for stopping the number testing at n = 5. This ability to communicate math ideas in writing is the peak indicator of achieving the learning objectives.

## Cognitive Traps for Students and Pedagogical Mistakes by Teachers

In daily teaching practice, failure to solve **critical thinking given an equation** problems is often not caused by students' ignorance of exponent topics. The main problem actually stems from inaccurate thinking habits and overly mechanical grading methods by teachers.

### Three Common Student Mistakes
1. **The Single-Answer Bias:** Since elementary school, students are used to routine problems that only have one single solution. When they find the combination m = 6 and n = 2 and prove it correct (6 - 2 = 4), they immediately stop looking. They skip the second instruction asking for the total number of possible solutions.
2. **Ignoring Domain Restrictions:** Students successfully derive the formula m = n + 4, then immediately write down the pairs (10, 6), (11, 7), and so on. They focus too much on the correctness of the mathematical operation and forget the maximum limit of 9 written in the problem sentence.
3. **Symbolic Operation Errors:** Students who have not mastered basic exponent concepts often fall into the trap of equating the division of exponential numbers with direct division of the powers. They assume (5ᵐ)/(5ⁿ) = 5⁴ can be simplified into m/n = 4, leading them to choose the pair m = 8 and n = 2.

::warning
If you find students writing m/n = 4, do not immediately blame them or simply tell them to memorize the formula aᵐ : aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ. Bring their understanding back to the concept of repeated multiplication to build meaningful learning. Ask them to expand (5⁸)/(5²) on scratch paper, then count how many 5s are left after simplifying. Let them prove for themselves that the result is 5⁶, not 5⁴.
::

### Two Pedagogical Errors by Teachers
On the other hand, we as teachers also need to evaluate our teaching methods in class. There are two habits that accidentally block students' potential for critical reasoning:
* **Algorithmic Spoon-Feeding:** Driven by curriculum material targets, teachers often immediately provide algebraic manipulation shortcuts before students recognize the pattern of relationships between variables. Once the problem is displayed and the class goes quiet for a moment, the teacher directly writes m - n = 4 on the board. This action takes away the students' opportunity to perform independent interpretation and analysis.
* **Clerical Scoring:** Teachers check student answer sheets only by looking at the bottom result without scoring the logical sequence of proof. If "5 solutions" is written, the student immediately receives a perfect score even without including a reason. In contrast, students who neatly arrange test tables but make a minor addition error at the end actually get a low score.

To maintain grading objectivity, use an analytical assessment rubric based on the four Facione indicators when checking critical thinking assignments:

| Assessment Criteria (Facione Indicators) | Score 1 (Needs Guidance) | Score 2 (Sufficient) | Score 3 (Proficient) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Interpretation & Analysis** | Incorrectly applies exponent properties (e.g., dividing powers directly). | Successfully changes the equation to m - n = 4 without writing boundary conditions. | Accurately writes exponent properties, the equation m - n = 4, and notes the limit 1 ≤ m, n ≤ 9. |
| **Evaluation (Solution Exploration)** | Only mentions 1 correct pair of (m, n) values. | Finds several value pairs, but misses some or breaks limits. | Systematically finds all valid value pairs without breaking limits. |
| **Inference & Explanation** | Writes the number of solutions without giving any reason at all. | Concludes the correct number of solutions, but the reasoning is unclear. | Accurately concludes the number of solutions accompanied by a logical reason related to the variable upper limits. |

::tip
Train students to note or circle domain constraints explicitly before starting calculations. Every time they face word problems or **critical thinking given an equation** instructions, ask students to circle the number conditions (like "between 1 and 9 inclusive" or "positive integers") using a different colored pen. This simple visual habit is highly effective at preventing students from picking numbers outside the allowed range.
::

::conclusion
Facing **critical thinking given an equation** instructions requires a major change in perspective, both for students doing the work and teachers acting as guides. A mathematical equation is not a one-way calculating machine, but rather a statement of logical balance that demands proof.

As a concrete step in the next class meeting, take one equation problem from the textbook, then remove its multiple-choice options. Change the instruction to an open-ended question that asks students to find all possible answer combinations within a specific number range, and use the Facione indicator-based rubric above to score the quality of their arguments. It is through these structured exercises that students' mathematical critical reasoning will be authentically sharpened.
